The Upper Story

A French startup just put a trillion-parameter model into the world as open weights, and a paper from Meta explains why the next breakthrough in robotics and autonomous agents isn't more compute — it's learning to look at the same problem from multiple heights at once.

The one thing

Mistral AI, the Paris-based lab, launched Mistral Large 4 on October 6 — nicknamed "Le Chonk" by its own team. It is a mixture-of-experts model with 1.05 trillion total parameters (only about 49 billion active per token), plus vision input, and a context window of up to a million tokens. Open weights arrive at the end of October. That is a meaningful milestone: a non-US lab releasing a model this size under an open licence, built largely on European infrastructure. On vendor benchmarks, it scores near the top of the second tier — not quite at the level of Anthropic's Fable family or GPT-6 Astra, but firmly competitive, and notably strong on cybersecurity tasks where it reportedly scores 93% on Cybench. Independent evaluators place it around the same level as DeepSeek 4.1 Flash — a capable model available for anyone to run.

The rest of the week, quickly

  • ResearchOpenAI published 722 manuscripts covering 372 result families in advanced mathematics — number theory, geometry, algebra, complexity theory — all generated by an unreleased internal model. A spokesperson said nearly all came from a single prompt to a single agent. The results have not been peer-reviewed, and OpenAI itself notes some "could have issues," but the sheer volume from one prompt is striking.
  • Open weightsGoogle DeepMind shipped EmbeddingGemma 2 under Apache 2.0 on October 6: a 740-million-parameter model that maps text, code, images, audio, and video into one shared vector space. It runs on a phone (about 567MB RAM for the full multimodal version), which makes high-quality semantic search possible on device with no cloud call.
  • PricingAnthropic launched Claude Haiku 5.5 at $0.10 per million input tokens and $0.50 per million output tokens — roughly 75% cheaper than Haiku 4.5. The price cut maintains the pattern of last year's capabilities trickling down to this year's bargain tier.
  • AgentsOpenAI reported benchmarks for GPT-6 Astra on legal, commercial, and procurement workflows: 55.0% task completion versus 41.6% for the previous model, with average time per task falling from 37 minutes to 19 minutes. Early real-world numbers, not lab benchmarks.

Paper, explained: the robot that learned to look before it stepped

H-JEPA: End-to-End Learning of Hierarchical World Models for Visual Planning · Wancong Zhang, Basile Terver, Michael Rabbat, Yann LeCun, Randall Balestriero (Meta AI Research / FAIR) · submitted 5 October 2026 · arXiv:2610.06805

Yann LeCun, one of the three people most responsible for modern deep learning, has spent several years arguing publicly that the field is building the wrong thing. Language models that predict words, image models that reconstruct pixels — all of it, he says, is predicting the wrong level of detail. The thing you actually want a machine to predict is meaning, compressed and abstract. H-JEPA is the clearest test yet of whether he is right.

The problem, and why it's hard

Robots fail at long-horizon tasks — navigating a building, assembling furniture, working in a warehouse — at a rate that still embarrasses the field. A robot might handle a single turn fine, then walk into a wall on move seven, because it had no plan beyond the next step. The obvious fix, planning further ahead, runs into a problem: the further into the future you predict, the noisier and harder that prediction becomes. A robot that tries to predict exactly what its camera will see twenty steps from now will get it wrong almost every time, and wrong predictions make wrong decisions.

The prior state of the art used something called a flat JEPA (Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture) — a single prediction model trained to forecast the future in a compressed internal representation rather than raw pixels. That helps compared to pixel prediction, but it still asks one model to answer questions at every timescale: what happens in one second, ten seconds, and a minute. Those questions need very different kinds of attention, and one model doing all of them inevitably makes compromises.

How it works

H-JEPA builds a stack of prediction models, each tuned to a different timescale. The analogy that makes this click: think of planning a road trip. A travel executive thinks in days — "we need to be in Chicago by Thursday." A route planner thinks in hours — "take I-80 West, stop for fuel near Iowa City." A driver thinks in seconds — "merge right, slow down for the curve." Each layer handles only what it can see clearly, and each layer's conclusion becomes a concrete target for the layer below. The driver doesn't need to know about Chicago; the driver just needs to make the next turn.

In H-JEPA, the layers work the same way. The top layer forecasts far into the future in a very compressed, abstract space — it strips away everything that might change fast (exact pixel values, small positional jitter) and keeps only slow, goal-relevant structure. That forecast becomes a subgoal: a target in compressed space that the middle layer must plan to reach. The middle layer in turn generates a more detailed subgoal for the bottom layer, which handles moment-to-moment movement. Each layer predicts in its own latent space — its own internal language — rather than in raw pixel space, so the predictions are inherently more stable and accurate at their respective timescales.

One crucial ingredient: the layers are trained end-to-end together, not separately. The top layer's compressed representation has to be genuinely useful for the bottom layer's planning, which means the whole stack co-adapts. You can't just stack off-the-shelf components; they have to learn to work as a hierarchy.

What they actually did

The researchers built a three-level hierarchy and tested it across four simulated environments covering navigation and manipulation tasks. The most dramatic result came from Visual AntMaze — a simulated ant-shaped robot that has to navigate a maze using only camera input, no privileged position sensor. A flat single-level JEPA solved 18% of trials. The three-level H-JEPA solved 73% of the same trials while using less planner compute — the hierarchy didn't need to search as broadly at each step because the goal passed down from above was already cleaner and more informative.

They also tested on DROID, a dataset of real robot manipulation videos collected by Stanford's robotics lab. Here the question was whether the learned hierarchical representations transfer to predicting outcomes on footage of actual robot arms in actual kitchens and labs — footage the model had never trained on. The hierarchical representations improved offline planning fidelity compared to the flat baseline, suggesting the learned structure generalises beyond simulation.

What they found

Two things drove almost all the gain: the temporal decomposition (letting different layers handle different timescales) and the goal representations passed downward. Ablate either one — remove the timescale separation, or stop passing high-level goals down — and performance drops back toward the flat baseline. The two factors together, not either alone, are what make the hierarchy work.

There was also a clean failure mode to note: if the timescale at each level is set wrong — for instance, if the middle layer tries to predict at roughly the same horizon as the top — the gains largely disappear. The hierarchy helps only if each layer is genuinely operating on a different chunk of time. That matters for practitioners hoping to apply this: you can't just add layers and expect improvement. The timescales have to be meaningfully different.

The catch

Everything here is either simulated or offline. The Visual AntMaze result is striking, but a simulated ant in a clean maze is a long way from a physical robot handling unexpected obstacles in a real room. The DROID experiments are encouraging but they measure planning fidelity on recorded video, not live control. There is also no comparison to other recent hierarchical planning methods beyond the flat JEPA baseline, so it is hard to know exactly where H-JEPA sits in the current landscape.

If you remember one thing: Planning in layers — short-term, medium-term, long-term, each in its own compressed language — dramatically outperforms planning in one flat horizon, and now there are numbers to show it.

Why it matters

Three things happened this week that look separate but share a structure. Mistral built a model with a trillion parameters but activates only a fraction of them per query — different "experts" handle different inputs, which is its own kind of hierarchy. H-JEPA's robots improved not by growing larger but by reorganising their planning into levels that each handle the right scope. OpenAI's math agent generated nearly four hundred result families from a single prompt — the level of the question, it turns out, matters as much as how hard you think. The pattern across all three: brute-force, flat approaches are giving way to architectures that know when to zoom in and when to zoom out. That shift is subtle, but it is showing up in the results.

Decoder

Word of the day
Latent space
The internal compressed map a model uses to represent meaning. If "dog" and "puppy" end up close together in this map, the model has understood they're similar — not because someone told it, but because it learned the geometry of meaning from data. H-JEPA's insight is to plan inside this map rather than in raw pixel space.
JEPA
Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture. LeCun's alternative to predicting every pixel: predict in a compressed, abstract space instead. Throws away noise, keeps meaning.
Mixture of Experts (MoE)
A model split into specialist sub-networks ("experts"). For each input, only a few experts activate — so the total model can be enormous while each individual forward pass stays cheap. Mistral Large 4 is MoE.
Embedding
Turning words, images, or audio into a list of numbers in which similar things land close together. EmbeddingGemma 2 puts all five modalities — text, code, images, video, audio — into one shared number space.
Open weights
A model whose internal numbers (parameters) are released for anyone to download and run. Open weights ≠ open source — the training code and data may still be private — but it does mean you can run the model without paying the lab.
Inverse dynamics
Working backward: given that something changed, what action caused it? H-JEPA uses this to learn from real-robot videos without being told what the robot was trying to do.
