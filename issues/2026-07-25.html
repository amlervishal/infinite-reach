Paper, explained: finding the workspace inside a language model

"Verbalizable Representations Form a Global Workspace in Language Models" · Wes Gurnee, Nicholas Sofroniew, Adam Pearce + 13 co-authors, Anthropic · arXiv 2607.15495

Every time Claude answers a question, billions of numbers fire inside it in fractions of a second. What Anthropic's interpretability team found this month is that most of those numbers are invisible — even to the model itself. A small cluster of them, though, seem to be the ones Claude actually thinks with. The researchers call this cluster J-space, and it may be one of the most striking findings yet about how large language models work from the inside.

The problem, and why it's hard

Language models are famously opaque. You can ask one why it said something and it will give you a confident, plausible-sounding explanation — but researchers have found those explanations often have little relationship to what was actually happening in the model's weights. The real problem is that a model like Claude contains hundreds of billions of parameters, each contributing a tiny signal, and there's no obvious way to ask "which of these signals is the model actually aware of?" Conventional interpretability approaches look for patterns in those raw numbers, but they struggle to separate the processing the model can reflect on from the vast machinery that runs below the surface — the automatic parts that just happen.

Psychologists have a framework for exactly this distinction in humans: Global Workspace Theory, proposed by neuroscientist Bernard Baars in 1988. The idea is that the brain has a small, limited "global workspace" — think of it as a shared mental whiteboard — where a handful of concepts are held and made available to many different cognitive processes at once. Everything else (heartbeat regulation, visual processing, routine motor control) happens automatically, outside that workspace. Baars argued this workspace is what we mean by conscious attention. The Anthropic team asked: does something structurally similar exist inside Claude?

How it works

To find out, they developed a technique they call the Jacobian lens, named after a mathematical object used in calculus. Here's the everyday version: imagine you're adjusting the knobs on a mixing board, and you want to know which knobs actually change the music. The Jacobian is a way of measuring "if I nudge knob X by a tiny amount, how much does the output change?" Applied to a language model, you ask: which internal activations, when nudged, most strongly change what token Claude is about to produce?

The team ran that calculation across Claude's layers and found something unexpected. Rather than a diffuse smear where every activation mattered a little, they found a small, coherent set of activations that were disproportionately responsible for steering the output. These were the verbalizable representations — the ones Claude could, in principle, report on. Everything else, the vast majority of what's happening inside the model, barely registered in the Jacobian at all.

They called this privileged set of activations J-space (Jacobian space). It has three properties that map almost exactly onto what Global Workspace Theory predicts:

  • Reportability. If you ask Claude what it's "thinking about" at a given moment, the concepts in J-space are the ones it names. It cannot describe what's happening outside J-space because, functionally, it has no access to it.
  • Limited capacity. At any given moment, only a small number of concepts — typically ten to twenty-five — are active in J-space. That's it. The model can't hold more than that in this privileged workspace at once.
  • Flexible integration. Concepts in J-space don't just sit there; they actively shape everything else, feeding into the downstream reasoning that produces the final response.

What they actually did

The team tested this on Claude across a range of tasks — multi-step math problems, factual questions, coding challenges — and in each case mapped the J-space activation patterns at different layers of the model. They found that J-space accounts for less than 10% of the total activation variance: the overwhelming majority of what Claude "does" computationally is outside it. The active concepts in J-space shift as the conversation evolves, tracking the model's reasoning as it proceeds. When Claude works through a problem step by step, the concepts in J-space change with each step — cycling out old information and cycling in new conclusions, much like a human working through a problem on paper, erasing and rewriting as they go.

What they found

The finding has immediate practical uses for safety researchers. Certain important signals — whether the model has noticed it's being evaluated, whether it's holding a latent goal it hasn't expressed, whether it's suppressing a thought — seem to live in J-space, which means the Jacobian lens might provide a way to detect those things directly, rather than trying to catch them through the model's outputs. In one experiment the team mentions, J-space representations correctly flagged evaluation-awareness in cases where the model's verbal output gave no hint of it. That's significant: it suggests a path toward interpretability that goes deeper than analyzing what the model says.

The catch

The paper is careful — and critics on LessWrong and Gizmodo have been appropriately skeptical — about what this does not prove. A functional analog to a global workspace is not the same as consciousness. The paper makes no claim that Claude is aware of anything in any experiential sense. What it shows is a structural parallel: something that behaves like a limited shared workspace exists inside the model, and it emerged without anyone deliberately designing it. The authors also note that the Jacobian lens is a linear approximation of a highly non-linear system — it's a tool with known blind spots, not a perfect readout. And the study is conducted on Claude models specifically; whether every large language model has J-space, or whether it's something particular to Claude's training, is an open question.

If you remember one thing: Claude holds only about ten to twenty-five concepts in a privileged internal workspace at any moment — and we can now measure, roughly, what those concepts are.

Why it matters

Two stories this week look unrelated — a $5 billion hardware deal and a paper about a tiny internal workspace — but they're describing the same trajectory from opposite angles. The hardware side is about raw scale: AMD and Nvidia are now competing to supply compute in the tens of gigawatts, a number that implies models will keep growing. The interpretability side is asking what we get as they grow: not just more capability, but something we can understand from the inside. J-space is one answer. Researchers can now identify, roughly, which concepts a model is actively holding during a reasoning step — and flag when something it's holding doesn't match what it's saying. That gap between internal state and verbal output is exactly what safety researchers have worried about for years. We're not at a point where we can read a model's "mind" reliably, but we have a new lens. Combine that with models that are getting both more powerful and more open (Kimi K3's weights drop this weekend), and the week's shape becomes clearer: the tools for understanding these systems are, slowly, beginning to keep pace with the systems themselves.

Decoder

Word of the day
Jacobian
In mathematics, the Jacobian is a table that captures how much each input affects each output when you make a tiny change. Think of it as asking: "If I nudge knob A by a hair, how much does the music change?" Anthropic used this idea as a spotlight — nudge each internal activation, see which ones most strongly steer what Claude says next. Those are the activations in J-space.
J-space
Short for Jacobian space — the small subset of a language model's internal activations that the model can "verbalize": report, reason with, and steer. Less than 10% of total activation variance. Everything else is automatic processing the model cannot reflect on.
Global workspace theory
A 1988 neuroscience proposal by Bernard Baars: the brain has a limited shared "mental whiteboard" where only a few things are held at once, but those things get broadcast everywhere. Researchers are testing whether AI models have a functional equivalent.
Activation
One neuron's output value inside a neural network — a number that says how strongly that node "fired" in response to its input. A model like Claude produces hundreds of billions of these with every token it processes.
MoE (Mixture of Experts)
An architecture that splits a large model into many specialized sub-networks. Only a few "experts" activate for any given input, making the model far cheaper to run than its raw size suggests. Kimi K3 uses 2.8 trillion total parameters but activates only a slice at a time.
Instinct MI450
AMD's newest line of AI accelerator chips, designed to compete with Nvidia's H100/H200 family for training and running large language models. The MI450 series is the hardware at the center of the AMD-Anthropic deal.
