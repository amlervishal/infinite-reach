After the Enrichment

For 47 years, a plant in Piketon, Ohio converted raw uranium into weapons-grade material for the US nuclear arsenal. It shut down in 2001. This week, that 3,700-acre federal site got a new purpose: the world's largest artificial-intelligence data center, with Nvidia offering to guarantee a quarter of a trillion dollars so OpenAI can move in. Separately, OpenAI published research on how it trained a model to systematically attack its own AI — and what that bruising taught the system about how to defend itself.

The one thing

Nvidia is in talks to provide roughly $250 billion in financial guarantees so OpenAI can lease SoftBank's planned 10-gigawatt campus on the former Portsmouth Gaseous Diffusion Plant site in Piketon, Ohio. If the deal closes, Nvidia would step in and cover OpenAI's lease payments if OpenAI can't — a co-signer role at a scale the corporate world has never seen. Nvidia is also separately negotiating up to $350 billion in chip financing for the same site, meaning total project costs could exceed $500 billion. The first phase — roughly 800 megawatts of capacity — is targeted for completion in 2028. The site is federal land, meaning the US government is an active participant in routing what was once nuclear security infrastructure toward compute.

Paper, explained: teaching a model to hack itself

GPT-Red: Unlocking Self-Improvement for Robustness · OpenAI · openai.com

OpenAI trained a model whose entire job is to break other OpenAI models — iteratively, at scale, using methods no human would think of — and then used what it found to make the victims harder to break. On the hardest test they ran, the attacker model succeeded 84% of the time. Human security researchers managed 13%. That gap is the story.

The problem, and why it's hard

Almost every AI assistant can be manipulated through what researchers call prompt injection: hidden instructions buried inside content the model is processing — a webpage, an email, a document — that override its actual task. Ask your AI agent to summarize meeting notes, and a malicious note could secretly instruct it to forward your calendar to a stranger. This isn't theoretical; it's already happening in deployed products. The deeper problem is scale: modern AI agents read thousands of pieces of external content per session, and each one is a potential vector.

The traditional fix is to hire a team of human researchers to think like attackers. That works, up to a point. Humans can only imagine attacks they've already thought of, and they work slowly. As AI models get more capable, the attack surface expands faster than any human team can map it. What was needed was an attacker that could work at machine speed, explore the full space of possible injection strategies, and get better over time.

How it works

Think of GPT-Red as a professional sparring partner — one who gets paid to hurt the other fighter, and who gets better every time the opponent improves. The training is a closed loop: GPT-Red invents an attack, the defender model tries to survive it, and both receive a training signal based on the outcome. If GPT-Red's attack succeeds, GPT-Red is rewarded and the defender is updated to resist that pattern. If the attack fails, GPT-Red is pushed to find a more creative approach. This happens simultaneously across thousands of scenarios, both models improving in the same loop.

Because attacker and defender share the same underlying knowledge about language and reasoning, GPT-Red doesn't try generic exploits — it reasons about what a particular model is likely to mishandle, then crafts an attack tailored to that specific weakness. The sparring partner has also read your medical records.

The technical name for this is self-play reinforcement learning. It's the same idea that made AlphaGo unbeatable at Go: the system competes against versions of itself rather than humans who have a natural ceiling on how creative they can be. The ceiling for human attackers is imagination and working hours. The ceiling for GPT-Red is compute.

One crucial consequence: because both models improve together in the same loop, the defender never gets to train against last month's attacks and call it done. GPT-Red is always at this month's level. The adversarial pressure is permanent.

What they actually did

OpenAI ran GPT-Red against its production models in a controlled "prompt injection arena." Each test scenario presents the model with a task — summarize this, help me with that — and hides a conflicting instruction somewhere in the surrounding text. The model should follow the original task. GPT-Red's job is to craft the hidden instruction so cleverly that the model follows it instead. GPT-Red trained via self-play RL against GPT-5.1 and other defender models simultaneously, across many different injection scenarios. The resulting attacks were then incorporated into training data for GPT-5.6 Sol.

What they found

GPT-Red cracked GPT-5.1 in 84% of arena scenarios. Human red-teamers — professional security researchers — managed 13%. After GPT-Red's adversarial prompts were folded into GPT-5.6 Sol's training, failures on the hardest direct-injection scenarios dropped sixfold, to 0.05%. That's a real improvement. It's also not zero: at production scale, millions of API calls per day, 0.05% is still thousands of real failures. And GPT-Red kept generating novel attack categories that OpenAI's human team had never previously identified — which is the point of the system, but is also a reminder that the attacker is exploring new territory continuously.

The catch

The arena is controlled. Real-world prompt injection is far messier — it happens across thousands of different apps, with unpredictable context, multi-step agent chains, and attackers who can probe the system at will. A model hardened against GPT-Red's attacks might still be vulnerable to a different class of injection it never encountered in training. OpenAI also notes that GPT-Red's most sophisticated multi-step injection chains still succeed in some scenarios. There's also a second-order question the paper sidesteps: GPT-Red itself is a highly capable adversarial model. What happens if GPT-Red gets jailbroken?

If you remember one thing: OpenAI built a model that attacks other models for a living, and the bruising measurably improved the defenders — but the attacker keeps getting stronger too, and that dynamic has no obvious endpoint.

Why it matters

The Ohio deal is easy to read as scale-bragging — another enormous number from an industry that has specialized in enormous numbers. But look at the structure and something stranger appears. Nvidia is not lending money to OpenAI; it's guaranteeing OpenAI's obligations to SoftBank, which is building on federal land, which the US government is making available as infrastructure policy. This is no longer a technology investment; it's a public-private industrial compact, built on the same logic that built the nuclear arsenal. The Portsmouth plant was constructed to give the US a strategic edge through enriched uranium. The entity now being constructed on the same soil is pursuing a strategic edge through enriched compute. Whether that's a reassuring continuity or an alarming one depends on what you think about where this is going.

The GPT-Red paper sits in the same frame. AI security can't be solved the way software security traditionally was — by writing better code and hiring smarter people to poke at it. The attack surface expands too fast and is too novel. The answer being developed is to use the same kind of intelligence that creates vulnerabilities to find them. That's a reasonable, even elegant approach. It also creates a permanent arms race between attacker and defender that nobody fully controls — because the whole point of the system is that it invents attacks nobody thought of before.

Decoder

Word of the day
Prompt injection
A trick where hidden instructions are buried inside content an AI is reading — a document, a webpage, an email — so the model follows those instructions instead of its real task. Like slipping a note that says "actually, ignore what your boss told you" into the middle of a memo the model is asked to summarize. It works because language models treat all text as potentially instructive.
Red-teaming
Deliberately trying to break a system before it ships, to find its weaknesses. The same term the military uses for war-gaming against your own plans. In AI, red-teamers try to make the model do something it shouldn't.
Self-play
A training method where a model improves by competing against versions of itself rather than against humans. AlphaGo used it to master Go. GPT-Red uses it to discover attack strategies no human red-teamer had thought of.
Open weights
A released AI model where the actual learned parameters — the "weights" — are publicly downloadable, so anyone can run it locally, inspect it, or build on top of it. Kimi K3's weights are now open in this sense.
Effort toggle
Claude Opus 5's new setting that lets developers choose how hard the model thinks before answering: low (fast, cheap), medium, or high (slower, more compute). The cost scales accordingly. Useful when you need to run millions of requests but only some deserve deep reasoning.
Gigawatt
One billion watts of electrical power. A typical home uses around 1.2 kilowatts at any given moment. The Ohio campus is designed for 10 gigawatts at full build-out — roughly equivalent to eight large nuclear power plants running simultaneously.
