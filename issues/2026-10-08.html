Three Days to Post

When word got out that an AI model was about to claim a 24-year-old mathematical conjecture, the human researchers who had been working toward it for years raced to file their own proof first — attaching a note that it was not quite in its intended final form. Separately, a research team in Beijing demonstrated what it looks like when machines and mathematicians work together on an even older problem, carefully and deliberately, and finish in two weeks for $25,000.

The one thing

On October 6th, OpenAI released 722 AI-written math manuscripts. CEO Sam Altman highlighted four as the most significant: a claimed proof of the Unique Games Conjecture — a 24-year-old open problem about the hardness of certain optimization puzzles — along with a quasi-Riemann Hypothesis result, the Hodge Conjecture for CM abelian varieties, and a result called L=BPL. The logical steps inside the Unique Games proof have been Lean-verified, meaning a computer has confirmed that each inference follows from the last. But the natural-language writeups accompanying the proofs are, per mathematicians who've seen them, nearly unreadable — one described them as "written by someone on psychedelics." OpenAI's internal model attempted about 8,000 open problems and solved roughly 5%, at around three hours of compute each. The team of researchers who had spent years working toward the Unique Games result — Minzer, Fei, and Wang — found out before OpenAI's release. They filed their own 95-page proof in three days with a disclaimer: "this manuscript is mathematically complete but not in its intended final form." The machine didn't get there first. But it changed what "first" means.

Paper, explained: How to formalize a century-old proof with AI and $25,000

An AI-Assisted Formalization of the Poincaré Conjecture · Zhiyuan Zhang, Axel Delaval, et al. (Peking University / Beijing International Center for Mathematical Research) · arXiv:2610.08329

The Poincaré Conjecture is one of the most famous problems in mathematics. Its proof — completed by Grigori Perelman between 2002 and 2003 — is considered one of the greatest mathematical achievements of the past century. This week, a nine-person team demonstrated that you can now formalize the entire thing using AI, in about two weeks, for roughly $25,000. And the real story isn't the price tag — it's the method.

The problem, and why it's hard

Poincaré asked, in 1904: if you have a closed three-dimensional shape with no holes and no boundary, must it always be topologically equivalent to an ordinary sphere? ("Topologically equivalent" means you can stretch or squish it, but not tear it.) For a two-dimensional surface — like the skin of a ball — the answer is obviously yes, and you can prove it easily. For three-dimensional shapes, the problem defied every mathematician who touched it for 98 years. Perelman cracked it using Ricci flow — a set of equations that behave like heat diffusing through a geometric shape, smoothing out irregularities over time — a technique borrowed from physics. He published the proof in three papers, posted on arXiv without fanfare, and later declined both the Fields Medal and the $1 million Millennium Prize that came with solving one of the seven greatest open problems in mathematics.

But Perelman's proof, however brilliant, is written in expert shorthand. It runs to hundreds of dense pages and relies on deep geometric intuitions that even specialist mathematicians have spent years interpreting. A formal proof — rewritten in a language precise enough for a computer to verify automatically — is a completely different job. You cannot leave gaps. You cannot say "it is clear that." Every inference must be stated explicitly, in the flat literal language of a proof assistant like Lean 4. Previous attempts to formalize Perelman's proof ran for years and remained incomplete.

How it works

Think of the gap between Perelman's proof and a Lean formalization like the gap between a master chef's recipe and the code for an automated factory kitchen. The recipe says "season to taste" — the factory needs exact grams. The chef's recipe is brilliant and correct; it simply wasn't written for machines. Translating it is painstaking, unglamorous work.

The Peking University team's key insight was to turn that translation problem into a coordination problem. Instead of trying to hold the entire proof in mind and work through it sequentially, they wrote a blueprint first: a structured outline of the proof broken into approximately 90 discrete milestones. Each milestone came with a precise specification: exactly what definitions were needed, exactly what lemma or theorem it needed to state, and exactly what the Lean 4 statement should look like. Think of these milestones as the meal-prep tasks in the factory: "chop onions to 5mm cubes," "reduce sauce to 200ml." No vagueness, no judgment calls — just a clearly specified job.

This blueprint structure had one crucial consequence: the 90 milestones could be worked on in parallel by different agents — some human mathematicians, some AI. An AI agent working on milestone 47 didn't need to understand milestone 72. It only needed to fill in that one precisely specified step correctly. Whenever an agent got stuck, the milestone's specification made it straightforward for a human mathematician to locate exactly where the problem was and provide targeted guidance — without having to reread hundreds of surrounding pages. The blueprint was the coordination mechanism. It replaced the need for any single mind, human or machine, to hold the entire proof in memory at once.

What they actually did

Preparation began in May 2026 with a team of three AI engineers with mathematical training, working alongside a group of mathematicians. Over the summer, the team developed the natural-language blueprint and formalized some background mathematical infrastructure — work that the Lean standard library (called Mathlib) didn't yet contain. The main formalization, using publicly available commercial AI models to fill in the ~90 milestones, restarted in September 2026 and took approximately two weeks. Total cost for AI subscriptions and server rentals: about $25,000. The paper covers both the topological and smooth Poincaré theorems — two related statements that together give the full result. The project's Lean code is publicly available at github.com/frenzymath/Poincare-Conjecture.

What they found

The formalization works — the Lean proof assistant accepts the proof — and the paper positions the project as a proof of concept for a general methodology: a well-structured blueprint can turn a years-long expert formalization effort into a two-week AI-assisted one. The paper also documents the kinds of human intervention that were still necessary: when an AI agent produced a logically valid but strategically misguided approach to a milestone, a mathematician needed to recognize the detour and redirect. The AI filled in gaps; it didn't design the structure. The paper also notes that a second independent team announced their own formalization of the same theorem around the same time — a sign that this kind of project has become tractable enough to attract parallel efforts.

The catch

The paper explicitly describes itself as a starting point, not a finished product. The background mathematical libraries the team had to build from scratch — geometric analysis infrastructure that Mathlib didn't have — are not yet mature enough for easy reuse on other projects. The methodology scales well here because there are detailed human-written expositions of Perelman's proof to follow. It's not yet clear how well it would work on a newer, less documented result where no such exposition exists. And there's a fundamental limit: this approach formalizes and verifies a proof; it does not discover one. Perelman's original insight still came entirely from a human.

If you remember one thing: A structured blueprint — 90 explicitly specified milestones — let AI agents work on a century-old theorem in parallel, turning a years-long formalization effort into two weeks and $25,000.

Why it matters

Mathematics has always been the frontier where humans set the definition of what counts as thinking. Two stories this week sit at opposite ends of a spectrum. OpenAI released hundreds of claimed proofs overnight, machine-generated at scale, where the gap between logical validity and human understanding is almost total — a Lean certificate says each step follows from the last, but nobody can yet read the argument. The Poincaré formalization took months of deliberate scaffolding: mathematicians designed the structure, AI filled in the pieces, and human judgment caught every strategic wrong turn. One is faster and more spectacular. The other is actually useful — it produces mathematics that humans can inspect, audit, and build on. Both will keep happening. The interesting question is which one the mathematical community develops infrastructure to absorb.

Decoder

Word of the day
Formalization
Taking a mathematical proof written in ordinary language — full of phrases like "it is easy to see" or "by standard arguments" — and rewriting every single step in a precise formal language that a computer can check automatically. Like converting a chef's recipe into factory-line code: nothing left to judgment, no step implied. The result isn't more mathematical; it's more machine-readable.
Lean 4
A proof assistant — a programming language where mathematical proofs can be written and then checked automatically by a kernel (a tiny, trusted piece of software). Think of it as a compiler for mathematics: it won't compile if the proof has a logical gap.
Unique Games Conjecture
A 2002 claim by mathematician Subhash Khot that a certain class of optimization puzzles is hard to even approximately solve. If true, it settles many open questions in algorithm theory about which problems are computationally hopeless. Still unverified as of October 8.
Poincaré Conjecture
The question of whether every closed 3D shape with no holes is topologically equivalent to a sphere. Proposed by Henri Poincaré in 1904; proved by Grigori Perelman in 2002–2003 using Ricci flow. One of seven Millennium Prize Problems; the only one ever solved.
Mixture of Experts (MoE)
An architecture where a model contains many specialized sub-networks ("experts") but only activates a small fraction for any given input. Mistral Large 4 has 1.05 trillion total parameters but uses only 49 billion at once — keeping compute cost manageable at a huge total size.
Lean-verified
A proof whose logical steps have been mechanically checked by the Lean proof kernel. It guarantees that each step follows validly from the last — but says nothing about whether the overall strategy is meaningful, readable, or useful to a mathematician.
