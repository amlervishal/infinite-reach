Before the Bell

Anthropic is heading for a public stock listing, its founders pledging most of their personal equity to philanthropy. A few miles away, OpenAI has quietly dialled back the ambition of its own fundraising round. The money is still arriving — it just wants more transparency now.

The one thing

Anthropic has filed to list on Nasdaq under the ticker symbol ANT, with a financing package reportedly worth $63 billion arranged by Broadcom to help the company buy the custom AI chips it needs to keep training frontier models. The same filing revealed that each of Anthropic's co-founders has pledged 80% of their personal equity in the company to philanthropic causes — an unusual and public commitment for a startup at this stage. Going public means quarterly disclosures, institutional shareholders, and analysts picking through every cost. That kind of scrutiny is a very different pressure from the private funding rounds that have sustained Anthropic so far. It is also a signal: the lab that has been most vocal about the risks of its own technology is now betting that public accountability will make it more trustworthy, not less.

Paper, explained: teaching a video model to take directions

Hy-Nomas: Towards Free-form Interactive World Generation · Tencent Hunyuan Visual Generation Team · arXiv 2610.36729

Most video generators work like a vending machine: you insert a text description, you collect a short clip. Hy-Nomas is a different kind of machine — one you can hold a conversation with. You can tell it to pan the camera, add rain, or make the character turn around, and each instruction builds on the scene it has already created. That capability turns out to require solving several hard problems at once, and the paper is a methodical account of how the team cracked them.

The problem, and why it's hard

Generating a single video clip from a text prompt is now well-understood. What remains difficult is interactive generation: letting a user steer the scene across multiple steps while keeping the world internally consistent. The challenge has two parts. First, a model has to track causality across time — what happened in frame 100 must still be logically true in frame 500, even if the user asked for a camera move in between. Second, it has to follow instructions that are genuinely "free-form": natural language like "make it more misty" is much harder to map to pixel changes than a precise API call. Before this work, interactive video models either locked the camera to fixed motion modes (left, right, zoom) or kept the scene so short that continuity wasn't really tested. Hy-Nomas tackles multi-turn, free-form interaction at a clip length that starts to resemble a real scene.

How it works

Think of it like a theatre director working with a troupe that has a very good visual memory. The director (you) says "it's a misty valley at dawn." The troupe improvises the opening scene. You say "pan right and show the village." They smoothly continue the scene — they remember what the valley looked like, they extend it. You say "now it starts to rain." They add rain consistently, without re-drawing the mountains. The "visual memory" that makes this work in Hy-Nomas is a design called action-aware causal attention. Standard video generators treat every frame equally; causal attention means each new frame can only look back at what came before, never forward, so the model is always extending a running history rather than revising it wholesale. The "action-aware" part means the model has been taught to connect specific kinds of instructions — camera moves, scene changes, object actions — to how pixels should shift. The training then happened in four stages: the model first learned to generate coherent video without any instruction (just visual causality); then it learned to follow text prompts; then it learned camera-control commands; finally it trained on its own outputs — video it had already generated — to get better at extending long sequences. This last stage, called diffusion distillation, is also what let the team shrink the final model enough to run on a single consumer GPU.

What they actually did

The base model is an 8-billion-parameter diffusion transformer built on top of HunyuanVideo 1.5, a Tencent video model that was already capable of high-quality single-shot generation. The team built a structured captioning pipeline — essentially a way to automatically annotate training videos with both what is happening and how the camera is moving — so the model could learn from large-scale unlabelled video rather than expensively hand-annotated clips. They then ran the four-stage curriculum above and, at the end, distilled the model into a lighter version that the paper says runs on a single RTX 4090 GPU with INT8-precision weights. They evaluated it on human preference studies comparing it to existing interactive video models, measuring whether outputs looked temporally consistent and whether the scene obeyed the user's instructions.

What they found

The team reports that Hy-Nomas outperformed existing interactive world models on their human-preference evaluation across both consistency and instruction-following metrics. The distilled model retained most of that quality at a fraction of the compute. One pattern worth noting: camera control commands worked more reliably than abstract scene-change instructions ("make it more cinematic" was harder than "pan left 30 degrees"). This aligns with what other researchers have found — geometric commands map more cleanly to pixel transformations than aesthetic ones.

The catch

The human-preference evaluation was conducted by the authors, not a third party, so the benchmark comparisons should be treated as preliminary until replicated. More practically: the paper demonstrates free-form interaction in relatively contained scenes. It is not yet clear how well the model handles very long interaction sequences (dozens of turns), rapid subject changes, or scenes with many distinct moving characters. The consistency that impressed evaluators in short sequences may degrade in longer ones. "Runs on an RTX 4090" is also relative — at INT8 precision, inference is slow enough that real-time interactive generation is not yet possible.

If you remember one thing: A video world model you can direct through conversation, rather than generate with a single prompt, is a meaningfully new kind of tool — and this is one of the first credible open-weight versions of it.

Why it matters

Two things are happening at once this week. The first is about money: the frontier AI labs are moving from private funding — where you can avoid hard questions — to public scrutiny, where you can't. Anthropic going public on Nasdaq means quarterly earnings calls, analyst days, and investors who want to know how soon "safety-first AI" turns a profit. OpenAI dialling back its own fundraising is a subtler version of the same pressure: the market is finally starting to ask for evidence alongside ambition. The second thing is about capability: models are beginning to simulate and steer persistent environments, not just answer questions. Hy-Nomas is one example; the cybersecurity focus of Gemini 4 Carbon is another. The tools are becoming less like calculators and more like environments you can step inside. Those two trajectories — public accountability, persistent AI environments — are going to collide in interesting ways over the next twelve months.

Decoder

Word of the day
World model
A system that has learned enough about how things work to simulate what would happen next, rather than just pattern-matching what has happened before. A chess engine that runs ahead by ten moves has a world model for chess. Hy-Nomas has a world model for how scenes look and change. The term comes from robotics, where robots need to plan actions by imagining consequences.
Causal attention
A design where each element in a sequence can only look at what came before it — not what comes after. It makes generation consistent across time: frame 200 can see frame 100, but not the other way around.
Diffusion distillation
Taking a slow, high-quality AI model and training a smaller, faster version to mimic it. The big model generates examples; the small model learns to match them, cutting compute without losing much quality.
INT8
A way of storing numbers that uses 8 bits instead of the usual 16 or 32. Models run in INT8 are roughly half the size and faster, at a small cost in precision — useful for fitting large models onto consumer hardware.
Frontier model
The current state-of-the-art — the most capable models that exist at a given moment, typically built by a handful of well-resourced labs. The frontier moves; what is frontier today is mid-tier in a year.
IPO
Initial public offering — the first time a private company sells shares to the general public. It creates public shareholders, quarterly reporting requirements, and a market price anyone can look up. For an AI lab, it also means disclosing far more about your financials and governance than you had to before.
