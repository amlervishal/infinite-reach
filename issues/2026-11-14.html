What We Expect Now

Google's fastest model outscores a bigger sibling; Anthropic's IPO pricing starts Monday; and a study of a million AI matchups finds that models aren't declining — the people judging them have quietly raised the bar.

The one thing

Google released Gemini 4 Hydrogen on November 12 — the fast, lower-cost entry in the Gemini 4 family. The result surprised even close observers: Hydrogen outscored Gemini 4 Argon, the larger model it was supposed to sit below, on the standard intelligence index used by independent evaluators. It runs 2.5× faster than Google's previous Flash model and claims 5× fewer fabricated answers. On a benchmark that tests whether a model can autonomously complete software-engineering tasks, it cleared 57 % — up from 18 % for the previous Flash. The lesson seems to be that for every model that scales up, there is a moment when a smaller, better-tuned one overtakes it on the metrics that actually get used in practice.

Paper, explained: the hidden drift in AI evaluation

"AI Isn't Getting Dumber, We're Getting Pickier: Rising Standards in Human Evaluation of Large Language Models" · Zihan Chen, Lei Nico Zheng, Di Zhu · arXiv: 2611.14333

You have probably heard someone complain that ChatGPT "used to be better." Maybe you've said it yourself. This paper tests whether that feeling corresponds to anything real — and finds that it mostly doesn't. The models haven't gotten worse. The people judging them have gotten pickier.

The problem, and why it's hard

Measuring how good a language model is turns out to be genuinely difficult. Automated benchmarks (multiple-choice tests, math puzzles) can be gamed, and the best ones get saturated quickly — a model scores 95 % and there's nothing left to distinguish it from the next one. So the community has leaned heavily on human preference evaluations: show two anonymous AI answers side by side, and ask a human to pick the better one. The leading platform for this is Chatbot Arena, run by researchers at Berkeley, where anyone can visit, chat with two hidden models simultaneously, and vote. The accumulated votes get turned into a live leaderboard.

The assumption baked into this method is that "human preference" is a stable measuring stick. If Model A beats Model B today and also beats Model B six months from now, the leaderboard means something. But what if the humans doing the voting are gradually changing their own standards — without anyone noticing?

How it works

Think of it like wine critics. Imagine the same bottle of wine is opened for a panel of critics once a month for fifteen months. The wine doesn't change — it's sealed, stable, identical. But the critics are going to wine tastings, reading reviews, talking to other experts. Each month they come back a little more experienced, a little more demanding. By month fifteen, the same wine that earned "pretty good" in month one earns "ordinary." Nothing changed except the judge.

That's the analogy at the heart of this paper. The researchers looked at the specific Chatbot Arena vote option that says "both are bad" — not "A won" or "B won," but a rejection of both choices. That vote is a signal of rising standards: the voter isn't choosing between two options; they're dismissing them. If voter standards were stable, the rate of "both are bad" votes should fluctuate randomly around a constant. If standards are drifting upward, that rate should climb steadily — even for models that haven't changed.

To test this, the researchers used fixed model versions. These are model snapshots that Chatbot Arena freezes in place, so a vote on a fixed version truly is a vote on the same model every time — not a model that the lab has been quietly updating. Any change in votes on a fixed version must come from somewhere else. The only candidate is the voter.

What they actually did

The team analyzed 1,260,967 blind votes cast on Chatbot Arena between May 2023 and August 2024. For the fixed-version models in that pool, they tracked the monthly rate of "both are bad" votes. They controlled for model, prompt category, and time of day, using a regression that isolates the time trend from other confounders.

What they found

The "both are bad" rate was rising by 1.71 percentage points per month, on a baseline of about 17.1 % — a roughly 10 % relative increase per month. By 2024, the monthly drift had steepened to 1.91 percentage points. This is happening on the same, frozen models. The models didn't get worse. The panel got harder to please.

The implication for the leaderboard: a model that was genuinely state-of-the-art in early 2023 would be rated significantly worse if first evaluated in mid-2024 — not because anything changed, but because the voters comparing it to the best current models had recalibrated their expectations upward. Apparent decline was largely an artifact of an evolving measuring stick.

The catch

The study covers May 2023 to August 2024. Models have improved enormously since then, and so the gap between early-era models and current ones is now much larger — which makes it harder to attribute any observed ratings decline solely to judge drift. The analysis also can't fully separate evaluation drift from possible changes in Chatbot Arena's user population over time: a site that starts niche and goes mainstream will attract different kinds of voters. And the study doesn't claim models never get worse — just that the drift in human standards is a real, measurable, and previously uncounted factor that makes it harder to know when they do.

If you remember one thing: When you say a tool "used to be better," part of what you're measuring is you.

Why it matters

The thread running through today's stories is a question about measurement: how do we know when something is actually getting better or worse, and when are we just moving the goalposts? Gemini 4 Hydrogen outscoring the larger Argon shows that the benchmarks used to rank models can flip with each new release — the smaller model winning on the metric that matters most this month. The Chatbot Arena paper shows the same dynamic in human judgment: the same model, frozen in time, looks worse as the voters around it evolve. And Anthropic is days away from letting public markets render a third kind of judgment — one that will be updated in real time, by millions of people who have all been watching the AI wave for years and grown considerably harder to impress. The bar keeps moving, and the systems we build keep chasing it.

Decoder

Word of the day
Evaluation drift
When the people scoring a system quietly upgrade their own standards over time — so the system looks like it's declining even if nothing about it has changed. The issue is in the judge, not what's being judged. Wine critics who keep going to tastings will eventually find the same bottle ordinary.
Chatbot Arena
A web platform where users compare two AI answers side by side — without knowing which model produced each — and pick a winner. The accumulated votes form a live leaderboard that the industry treats as a rough measure of real-world quality.
Hallucination
When an AI model confidently states something factually wrong. Called a hallucination because the model produces an answer that isn't grounded in anything real — it fills a gap with plausible-sounding invention.
Agentic
Describes a model that works through a task in steps — writing code, running it, reading the output, trying again — rather than just producing a single response and stopping. The model acts more like a problem-solver than a search engine.
Coverage collapse
A failure mode in model fine-tuning where the model gets so focused on being rewarded for one type of response that it loses flexibility everywhere else — like a musician who practices one piece so much they can't improvise.
Inference throughput
How many responses an AI system can produce per second. Higher throughput means lower latency for users and lower cost per query for the company running the model.
