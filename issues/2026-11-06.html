What the Barrel Holds

A four-decade mystery about a barrel-shaped protein cage in living cells met its answer this week — found in days rather than years, with an AI acting as an unusually tireless research apprentice. Elsewhere, OpenAI quietly handed a cybersecurity model to a small group of vetted testers, and a new benchmark reminded everyone that "understanding" a document and remembering its moving parts are very different things.

The one thing

Vault particles are barrel-shaped protein cages found inside cells. Scientists first spotted them in the mid-1980s and have puzzled over them ever since: they are among the largest protein structures in the cell, they appear in almost every organism studied, and no one has firmly established what they do. What was clear is that only complex cells — plants, animals, fungi — were known to build them. This week Anthropic's biology team announced it had used Claude to change that. The AI searched roughly 15 billion predicted protein structures, found vault-like proteins in bacteria and bacteriophages (viruses that infect bacteria), helped design the experiments to test them, and assisted with the cryo-electron microscopy that produced the first three-dimensional map of a prokaryotic vault — the kind built by simpler cellular life. The clearest structure, assembled from a phage protein called L05, resolved to 3.2 ångströms (fine enough to see where individual atoms sit) and is built from 96 identical protein copies. A preprint has been submitted to bioRxiv. The vault's job in the cell remains unknown.

Paper, explained: the barrel that baffled biologists for forty years

Investigating vault particles with Claude · Anthropic Biology Team · Anthropic announcement · preprint submitted to bioRxiv

Scientists have known about vault particles for more than forty years and still can't agree on what they're for. When Anthropic's biology team used Claude to find and map them in prokaryotes — the simplest living cells — it felt like watching a cold case suddenly crack open.

The problem, and why it's hard

Vault particles were discovered by accident in the 1980s, when researchers using electron microscopes noticed odd barrel-shaped cages floating in cell extracts. They are huge — among the top five largest protein structures found inside cells — and they turn up in nearly every eukaryote studied. (Eukaryotes are organisms whose cells have a nucleus: everything from yeast to humans.) Their size, their ubiquity, and their strange shape suggested they were important. But four decades of research have not nailed down what they actually do in a living cell. Some studies linked them to drug resistance in cancer; others to immune signalling. Nothing stuck.

What nobody had found was a vault in prokaryotes — the simpler branch of cellular life that includes bacteria. A prokaryotic cell has no nucleus and a much more streamlined toolkit; it was generally assumed to lack the machinery to build something as elaborate as a vault. Finding one would rewrite the evolutionary history of these structures, suggesting they predate the split between simple and complex cellular life by hundreds of millions of years. The barrier wasn't conceptual; it was practical. Looking for vault-like proteins across all of known biology means scanning billions of protein structures — and without a machine to do that scanning, the search was simply out of reach for a human team.

How it works

Think of proteins as three-dimensional objects that snap together based on their shapes, the way LEGO bricks click when their studs match. If you want to find all the bricks in the world that could slot into a particular gap, you need to look at billions of bricks one by one — or build a machine that recognises the pattern instantly. That is roughly what the team did.

Claude searched a database of roughly 15 billion predicted protein structures, hunting for proteins with the geometric features of a vault subunit. It did not stop at the search. Claude also suggested which candidates were worth testing experimentally, helped design the DNA sequences needed to produce those proteins in the laboratory, proposed a purification protocol to isolate them in quantity, and ran the cryo-EM processing software that turns raw experimental images into a usable 3D model. The human researchers set the scientific direction and checked every output — but the machine handled the searches that would otherwise have taken weeks and the protocol iterations that eat up laboratory time.

Cryo-electron microscopy works by flash-freezing a protein sample so fast that no ice crystals form, then bombarding it with electrons. The electrons scatter differently depending on the density of whatever they hit, and thousands of 2D images taken from different angles can be computationally assembled into a 3D structure. The resolution of that model is measured in ångströms — one ångström is 0.1 nanometres, roughly the width of a hydrogen atom. Getting to 3.2 Å is good enough to see where the protein's backbone sits and where individual side-chains point, which is the level of detail needed for real structural biology.

What they actually did

The team ran cryo-EM on four candidate proteins and confirmed that all four formed vault-shaped particles. Two candidates came from bacteria; two came from bacteriophages. The clearest structure came from a phage protein called L05: its vault is built from 96 copies of a single protein arranged in a symmetrical barrel, resolved to 3.2 Å. Claude's initial search covered roughly 15 billion predicted protein sequences in the database. Protein purification — a step that typically takes around three days when designing a protocol from scratch — dropped to approximately 90 minutes using the protocol Claude suggested and the team validated. The whole project, from computational search to experimental confirmation, ran in days rather than the weeks or months a manual equivalent would have required.

What they found

They found that vaults are older and more widespread than the field had assumed. Bacteria and bacteriophages can build vault-shaped particles, which suggests the structure likely predates the split between simple and complex cellular life — an event that happened over a billion years ago. The L05 structure at atomic resolution also gives researchers a new starting point: the new prokaryotic vault can now be compared against the known eukaryotic vaults to identify which features are conserved across evolution (usually a sign those features are functionally important) and which differ.

Anthropic acknowledged that Claude made mistakes along the way. One was a two-week protocol error that the human team caught before it caused major loss of time; another was generating a mirrored map — a structure that is the mirror image of the correct one, a classic cryo-EM artefact — which a researcher identified and corrected. Neither error made it into the final results, but both are honest reminders that the tool is an assistant, not an authority.

The catch

This is a preprint, not yet peer-reviewed, so independent confirmation is still ahead. The vault's biological function remains completely unknown — this paper maps the shape, not the purpose. It is also worth being clear about what "Claude helped" means here: the AI was used as a search engine over a large database, a protocol suggester, and a software operator, not as a reasoning system that understood the biology in the way the human researchers did. The researchers understood the biology; Claude handled the combinatorial searches that were previously too costly to run. The structure data has also not yet been deposited in public databases, which means other labs cannot build directly on the atomic model until that step is complete.

If you remember one thing: An AI-assisted search found a 40-year-old mystery hiding in the simplest living cells — the shape is now known, but the vault's purpose in life remains a mystery for the next chapter of research.

Why it matters

The vault result is a small story with large implications. Structural biology normally moves in a measured rhythm: identify a target, spend months on experimental design, run samples, wait for results. Claude compressed the search-and-design phase from weeks to days and the purification step from three days to ninety minutes — which is a change in kind, not just speed. Teams can now afford to ask questions they would not have considered worth the time before. The vault's unknown function may turn out to be medically significant: earlier work in complex cells linked vaults to multidrug resistance in cancer. That research thread can now be explored across a much wider set of organisms than anyone had mapped before. The fact that Claude made errors — a protocol mistake, a mirrored reconstruction — matters equally: it confirms that this is a tool that augments a skilled scientific team, not one that replaces the need for people who understand what they are looking at. The wins belong to both sides of that collaboration.

Decoder

Word of the day
Cryo-EM
Short for cryo-electron microscopy. You flash-freeze a protein sample so fast it can't form ice crystals, then hit it with electrons to take thousands of images from random angles. A computer assembles those 2D snapshots into a 3D model — a bit like reconstructing a building from millions of photographs taken by people walking randomly around it in the dark.
Vault particle
A barrel-shaped protein cage found inside nearly all complex cells, first spotted in the 1980s. About three times the size of a ribosome. Function: still unknown after forty years of research.
Prokaryote
A cell without a nucleus — the simpler branch of life, including all bacteria. Opposite of eukaryote (animals, plants, fungi). The two groups diverged more than a billion years ago.
Angstrom (Å)
A unit of length equal to one ten-billionth of a metre. Used in structural biology to describe how fine a 3D model is. 3.2 Å is roughly the distance between two bonded carbon atoms — good enough for real structural analysis.
Bacteriophage
A virus that infects bacteria rather than animal cells. Sometimes shortened to "phage." The L05 protein that produced the clearest vault structure came from one of these.
Sparse MoE
Mixture of Experts — a model architecture where only a portion of the network activates for any given input. A 280B MoE model might use only ~14B parameters at a time, keeping it cheap to run while keeping the total model large and capable.
