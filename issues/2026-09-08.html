The narrative around this era has been shaped entirely by announcements. The S-1 will be the first time numbers replace press releases.

Paper, explained: Teaching a reasoning model when to stop

"Your Models Have Thought Enough: Training Large Reasoning Models to Stop Overthinking" · Jinyi Han, Ying Huang, Ying Liao et al. · arXiv 2509.23392

Today's most capable AI models are expensive partly for a reason nobody anticipated: they think too long. Not wrong — just redundantly. A fresh paper this month proposes a training method called JET (Just-Enough Thinking) that teaches models to stop reasoning exactly when they have enough evidence, and the results include something rare in efficiency research: fewer tokens and higher accuracy at the same time.

The problem, and why it's hard

The breakthrough behind OpenAI's o-series, DeepSeek-R1, and their successors was training AI models to reason step by step before answering. Show the model thousands of examples of worked solutions, reward it when the reasoning leads to the correct answer, and it learns a general habit of careful, extended thinking. That habit is genuinely useful — hard math problems, complex code, multi-step planning all benefit from it.

But researchers started noticing a strange pattern: if you read the reasoning chains these models produce, the answer is often effectively settled quite early. The model "knows" on step 8 what the answer is. It then spends steps 9 through 25 restating, double-checking, and re-deriving what it already had — before finally writing the final answer. This is called overthinking. The waste isn't just philosophical. Every reasoning step is a token, and tokens cost compute time and money. A model that generates 800 unnecessary tokens per question multiplies infrastructure costs with no accuracy gain.

The deeper problem: existing reinforcement-learning training methods cannot easily fix this. During training, the model explores many possible reasoning paths (called rollouts). Short paths — even correct ones — are statistically rare compared to longer ones. So the model never learns that short paths are equally valid. Penalising length directly tends to make the model cut reasoning that it actually needed, hurting accuracy.

How it works

The researchers reached for an idea from cognitive psychology called evidence accumulation. When a person makes a decision under uncertainty — say, deciding which of two overlapping sounds is louder — they don't gather evidence forever. They accumulate it until internal evidence crosses a decision threshold, then commit. More evidence past that threshold adds noise, not signal. The question JET asks: can a language model be trained to behave the same way?

Think of it like a student taking an exam. A standard reasoning model is like a student who works out the correct answer at the bottom of page one, but keeps writing until the bottom of page three to "make sure." The last two pages are genuine — the student isn't being lazy — but they add nothing to the grade. JET trains the student to put down the pen when the work is done.

The mechanism has two parts. The first is trajectory truncation during training rollouts. When the training process samples a reasoning chain (a rollout), JET tests at each step whether cutting the chain there would still produce the correct answer. If so, it records that truncation point and uses the shorter chain as a training example. The model sees: "you could have stopped here and still been right." Over thousands of examples, it learns to stop at similar points in the future.

The second part is a quality-controlled length reward. Standard RL training rewards correctness. JET adds a second signal that rewards both correctness and brevity — but with a hard gate: if the shorter answer is wrong, no reward for brevity. This prevents the model from learning to be carelessly short. It only learns to be short when shortness doesn't cost accuracy. The two signals together — see shorter paths that work, get rewarded for taking them — constitute the training recipe.

What they actually did

The team ran experiments on DeepSeek-Distill-Qwen-1.5B, a 1.5-billion-parameter model built by distilling the reasoning style of the much larger DeepSeek-R1 into a smaller base. They evaluated on several mathematical reasoning benchmarks, with the headline test being the Olympiad benchmark — problems drawn from international mathematics competitions, which require genuine multi-step reasoning. They compared three conditions: standard RL fine-tuning, a simple length-penalty baseline (punish longer chains regardless of accuracy), and JET. They also tested JET across four different LLM backbones to check that the results weren't specific to one model family.

What they found

On the Olympiad benchmark, JET improved accuracy by 4.6 percentage points while reducing output length by 46.3%. That combination is the unusual finding. In most efficiency research, you choose: fewer tokens or better accuracy. JET achieves both. The length-penalty baseline, as expected, cut tokens but also cut accuracy — it removed useful reasoning along with redundant reasoning. JET's trajectory-truncation approach is more surgical: it removes only what genuinely wasn't needed.

The gains transferred across the other math benchmarks tested and across all four model backbones, suggesting the method captures something general about how reasoning models store and retrieve evidence — not a quirk of one architecture. The researchers attribute part of the accuracy gain to a training-distribution benefit: by exposing the model to shorter, still-correct chains, it learns cleaner and more consistent reasoning patterns overall, rather than picking up the verbose habits of training examples that happened to meander to the right answer.

One failure mode is worth naming: JET cannot distinguish "I know the answer" from "I think I know the answer but I'm wrong." On genuinely hard problems near or above the model's ceiling — where extended deliberation would actually help — premature stopping still costs accuracy. The savings are largest on problems the model can solve; on truly intractable ones, the full chain remains valuable.

The catch

All experiments here involve a 1.5-billion-parameter model. It remains untested whether the same evidence-accumulation dynamics hold at the scale of GPT-6 Astra or Claude Opus 5, which are orders of magnitude larger. The point at which a reasoning chain "has enough evidence" may look very different at 2 trillion parameters than at 1.5 billion — or it may look the same. This is genuinely unknown. JET also requires the ability to do live trajectory truncation during training rollouts, which is a non-trivial infrastructure change for labs doing training at scale. And the threshold for "enough evidence" was tuned on math problems; whether it transfers cleanly to code, scientific reasoning, or long-horizon agent tasks needs separate study.

If you remember one thing: Reasoning models often settle on the correct answer far earlier than they stop talking — and a training method borrowed from cognitive psychology can teach them to put the pen down at the right moment, saving nearly half the tokens without sacrificing accuracy.

Why it matters

This week's stories, taken together, trace the same underlying shift: the frontier AI era is entering a phase where efficiency matters as much as raw capability. GPT-6 Astra completes computer-use tasks in 40 minutes instead of 75 — not just better, faster. Qwen3.8-Max nearly triples its agentic benchmark without touching its architecture, just its post-training. The JET paper finds 46% token savings just by teaching a model when to stop. And Anthropic's IPO prospectus, when it lands, will reveal for the first time what that efficiency race actually costs — in infrastructure, talent, and compute. The transition from "how capable?" to "at what cost?" is one of the defining questions of the next few years, and this week the accounting begins.

Decoder

Word of the day
Overthinking
In AI, not a personality trait but a measurable inefficiency: a reasoning model that keeps generating thinking tokens after it has already accumulated enough evidence to give the correct answer. The extra tokens cost compute and money but add nothing to accuracy. Today's paper is about fixing it.
Evidence accumulation
A concept from cognitive psychology: decision-makers gather evidence until it crosses an internal threshold, then commit. Past the threshold, more evidence adds noise rather than signal. JET applies this idea to AI reasoning chains.
Trajectory truncation
JET's core training trick: cut a reasoning chain at the earliest point where the correct answer is already determined, and use that shorter chain as a training example. The model learns that stopping there is valid.
Large Reasoning Model (LRM)
An AI model specifically trained to produce step-by-step thinking before a final answer — the category including OpenAI's o-series, DeepSeek-R1, and similar systems. The reasoning steps are tokens; every token costs compute.
S-1 filing
The document a company submits to the US Securities and Exchange Commission before going public. It's the first audited, legally accountable look at the company's finances — revenue, margins, risk factors, and how the business actually works.
Rollout
In reinforcement learning training, a rollout is one complete run of the model attempting a task — generating a full chain of thought and an answer. JET intercepts the rollout mid-run when enough evidence has accumulated.
