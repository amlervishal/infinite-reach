Without a Map

Europe's regulators just called ChatGPT a search engine — the only legal map they had that fit. And a benchmark from Tsinghua University found that today's best AI research agents score only 27 out of 100 when researchers hand them a problem but don't tell them how to solve it.

The one thing

On August 31, the European Commission formally designated ChatGPT as a "Very Large Online Search Engine" under its Digital Services Act — the first time any AI assistant has been slotted into this regulatory category. ChatGPT had about 159 million monthly EU users, well over the 45-million threshold that triggers the designation. It's not that the EU thinks ChatGPT is literally Google; it's that ChatGPT's live web-search function makes it functionally equivalent under the law. OpenAI now has until late November to comply with a suite of transparency and risk-assessment obligations — including annual audits on everything from illegal content to effects on elections. Non-compliance can cost up to 6% of global annual revenue. Reddit and Roblox were added to a related category the same day. This is the first real test of whether the DSA can adapt to technology it was never designed for.

Paper, explained: Can AI do science without being told how?

ASI-Bench: At the Dawn of Artificial Superintelligence · Junwei Zhou, Zhen Sun, Binyu Li et al. (Tsinghua University) · arXiv:2608.17271

Here is a question that sounds obvious until you sit with it: if an AI system is genuinely good at science, should it need to be told how to do the science? A new benchmark from Tsinghua University put that question to 18 of the world's best AI agent configurations. The answer — 27 out of 100 when researchers withhold the method — is harder to spin than any benchmark chart you've seen recently.

The problem, and why it's hard

There is a widespread belief, not just in the press but among researchers, that AI is approaching the point where it can accelerate scientific discovery on its own. The evidence for this is real: AI systems have predicted protein structures, proposed new drug candidates, helped design materials, and written passable review articles. But almost all of these demonstrations share a hidden feature — a human expert decided what question to ask and, crucially, what method to apply. The question nobody had answered systematically was: what happens when you take that guidance away?

The challenge of answering this rigorously is that most existing benchmarks test narrow knowledge — a question with a right answer, a coding problem with a known solution. Real scientific research is different. It's a multi-step project that requires choosing among plausible methods, executing experiments that may fail, interpreting ambiguous results, and delivering something that wasn't there before. Measuring that kind of work demands tasks that look like actual research, not multiple-choice problems.

How it works

Think of ASI-Bench as a series of graduate-level research projects. Each project has a scientific objective — say, train a model that predicts a biological property from molecular structure, or run a simulation of a physical system and analyze what you find. The benchmark's key innovation is that each project comes in four versions, identical in objective but different in how much methodological guidance is provided. The authors call these guidance levels B1 through B4.

Imagine you've just started a PhD and your supervisor gives you a research problem. In the B1 version, they've handed you the full recipe: background reading, the specific method you should use, the equations, and the exact experimental procedure. You still have to execute it, but the path is clear. In the B2 version, they've told you the method's name and some constraints, but not how to carry it out. In the B3 version, they hand you only the objective, the dataset, and the required output — you have to figure out what method makes sense entirely on your own. In the B4 version, they add a twist: some of the context they provide is factually correct but irrelevant, designed to throw you off. The scientific goal and the evaluation criteria are exactly the same across all four versions. Only the map changes.

The benchmark covers 60 such projects across 11 scientific domains, including machine learning, biology, chemistry, mathematics, and robotics. Building it required more than 40 domain experts, over 31,000 hours of human effort, and more than 1,500 sandbox test runs to validate that the tasks were well-posed. The evaluation itself tracked not just whether the AI got the right answer, but how many steps it took, how many tools it called, and how far through the research pipeline it got.

What they actually did

The researchers ran 18 different combinations of agentic frameworks and underlying models through all four guidance levels. The configurations included leading closed-source models paired with research-grade agent harnesses designed for scientific tasks — systems set up to browse papers, write code, run experiments in a sandbox, and interpret their own results. Each task run involved hundreds of interaction turns and execution steps; the full evaluation consumed more than 35 hours of agent runtime in total.

What they found

With full guidance (B1), the best systems averaged 50.91 out of 100. That's a respectable graduate-student performance — not perfect, but genuinely useful. The cliff comes immediately. When guidance was reduced to just the method name (B2), the average score fell to 29.10. When only the objective was given (B3), it dropped further to 26.62. Every single B3 and B4 configuration scored below 40. The drop from "here's the method" to "figure out the method yourself" was the decisive moment — roughly 25 percentage points in average performance, not from one level to the next but from the first step to the second.

What the numbers obscure is the nature of the failures. The systems didn't just underperform; they often made confident-sounding wrong choices. When left to choose their own methods, agents frequently defaulted to whatever approach appeared most in their training data — not necessarily the most appropriate one for the specific task. They also struggled with the chain of decisions required over a multi-step project: an early wrong choice tended to compound rather than self-correct.

The catch

ASI-Bench is a powerful stress test, but it measures one specific dimension of scientific competence: the ability to execute a project-level task autonomously from scratch. It does not measure creativity in posing new research questions — arguably an equally important part of science. Nor does it capture collaborative research, where humans and AI trade off on different steps. The 60 tasks, though diverse, were constructed to be well-defined enough for automated evaluation; the messiest real science, where even the evaluation criteria are contested, is not in scope. And a B3 score of 26.62 may still represent genuine value: there are plenty of tasks where partial progress or approximate results matter.

If you remember one thing: Today's AI research agents perform about as well as a graduate student following a detailed recipe — and less than half as well when the recipe is taken away.

Why it matters

Two stories this week share a hidden theme: what it looks like when the world is still figuring out how to name something. Europe's regulators needed a legal category for ChatGPT, and the closest thing they had was "search engine" — not wrong, but not really right either. The technology moved faster than the taxonomy. The ASI-Bench paper reveals the same gap inside the models themselves: AI systems can follow a method expertly, but choosing the right method on an open-ended scientific problem — the thing that actually makes a scientist a scientist — remains elusive. The scores don't say AI is useless at science; they say it's at its best when someone has already done the hardest intellectual work. Getting from 27 to 50 without the map is probably the central research problem of the next few years.

Word of the day
VLOSE
Very Large Online Search Engine — the EU's DSA category for platforms with over 45 million monthly EU users that function like search engines. ChatGPT just became one. Think of it as the tier that gets the most homework: annual audits, algorithmic transparency reports, and access for vetted researchers.
DSA
Digital Services Act — the EU law that governs what large online platforms must do to stay legal. Different from the AI Act; this one is about content, search, and how platforms interact with their users.
Mixture of Experts (MoE)
A model architecture that splits a very large neural network into specialised "expert" sub-networks and routes each token to only a few of them. GLM-5.3's 743 billion parameters sound enormous, but only 18 billion are active on any given input — which is why these models can be fast and relatively cheap to run despite their headline size.
Agentic
Describes AI systems that take multi-step actions on their own — browsing the web, writing and running code, calling tools — rather than just answering one question and stopping. ASI-Bench's agents are agentic; they plan and execute entire research pipelines.
Benchmark
A standardised test used to compare AI models. Good benchmarks are hard to "game" and measure something that actually matters in the real world. ASI-Bench's design — same objective, four guidance levels — is specifically meant to prevent models from memorising the right answer rather than demonstrating genuine competence.
Guidance level
ASI-Bench's term for how much methodological detail a research agent is given. B1 = full recipe. B2 = method name only. B3 = just the goal. B4 = goal plus red herrings. Same objective, four different maps.
