After Ninety Years

Two teams published competing mathematical proofs of a question about flowing fluids that has stood since the 1930s. Both used AI to get there. Neither story is fully resolved — and the race for credit has already begun.

The one thing

On September 7, NYU mathematician Tristan Buckmaster and Harvard researcher Levent Alpöge (also affiliated with Anthropic) posted three preprints proving that certain fluid equations can break down — generate an infinite value — in finite time. Their proofs were verified in Lean, a computer program that checks every logical step against the axioms of mathematics and cannot miss a gap. Then, on September 8, OpenAI announced something bolder: a claimed solution to the full Navier-Stokes Millennium Prize Problem — one of only seven such prize questions in existence, each carrying a $1 million award, only one of which has ever been solved before. OpenAI says an internal system ran roughly 10,000 AI agents in parallel over 88 hours to produce the proof. That claim has not been independently verified by human mathematicians. The two results cover related but distinct equations. Expert review is underway. A dispute over credit and timing is already documented.

Paper, explained: Two proofs of a ninety-year question about flowing water

"Finite time blowup with smooth forcing for the 3D incompressible Euler equations" · Tristan Buckmaster & Levent Alpöge (NYU / Harvard–Anthropic) · statement and papers

Tucked inside one of the hardest open questions in mathematics is a deceptively simple worry: can smooth, well-behaved fluid motion ever collapse into a singularity — a value that goes to infinity? For roughly ninety years, no one could answer it. This week, two separate groups said they could, arriving within 24 hours of each other, with AI throughout and a dispute over credit already underway.

The problem, and why it's hard

The equations governing how fluids move — air over a wing, blood through an artery, the atmosphere we model weather in — come in two main versions. The Euler equations, written down in the 1750s, describe ideal frictionless flow. The Navier-Stokes equations, from the 1800s, add viscosity: the internal resistance that makes honey thick and water thin. Both models have powered engineering for two centuries.

The mathematical question is not whether fluids behave oddly in practice — they don't produce infinities in the real world — but whether the equations themselves are complete. Can a perfectly smooth starting configuration, evolved forward in time using only the equations' own rules, eventually produce an undefined, infinite value — a singularity? If it can, the equations have a hidden incompleteness. If it can't, they're mathematically stable forever. This question, called the regularity problem, has been one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems since 2000, with a $1 million reward attached to the Navier-Stokes version.

The difficulty is partly the equations' non-linearity — a fluid's future behaviour depends on its past in ways that don't simplify cleanly — and partly that proving a blowup means constructing one exactly: specifying a precise starting configuration from which the equations guarantee infinite values at some finite later time. You cannot just argue one might exist. You have to build it, in verifiable mathematics.

How it works

The everyday analogy is a spinning ice skater who pulls their arms in. As the arms come closer to the body, conservation of angular momentum demands the spin speed up — and if you imagine this process unchecked, with no friction and no physical body to stop it, the spin would increase without limit, reaching an infinitely tight, infinitely fast point in finite time. That is a mathematical singularity. That is what finite-time blowup means.

Buckmaster and Alpöge built on work by Spanish mathematicians Diego Córdoba and Luis Martínez-Zoroa, who had recently shown blowup for the incompressible porous medium (IPM) equation — a simpler cousin of Euler that governs flow through rocks. Buckmaster and Alpöge found a variation of that argument that extends to two harder systems: the 2D Boussinesq equations (which model warm-air convection, like a weather front rising) and the full 3D incompressible Euler equations.

The hardest technical challenge is the forcing term. In their setup, the equations receive a gentle external push — a smooth forcing — that helps drive the blowup. The mathematical obligation is to ensure this push stays genuinely smooth throughout, even as the solution develops a singularity. Previous attempts at related problems struggled here: the forcing became rough at exactly the moment it mattered most. Buckmaster and Alpöge found a construction where the smoothness is guaranteed all the way to the blowup time.

Then they did something unusual: they machine-verified all three proofs in Lean, a proof-assistant program used in formal mathematics. Lean checks each step of an argument against the axioms of logic. A human reader can miss an error buried in a hundred pages; Lean cannot. The Lean formalisation ran to hundreds of thousands of lines of formal code. The result is qualitatively more trustworthy than a journal preprint awaiting peer review.

They also disclosed the role of AI throughout: they used Anthropic's Claude to identify the key elements of the Córdoba–Martínez-Zoroa proof and to reproduce its argument, and used Claude and OpenAI's Codex to draft the text of all three papers. The humans directed the mathematical strategy; the models did much of the derivation and all of the prose.

What they actually did

Three preprints dropped September 7: one for the porous medium equation, one for the 2D Boussinesq system, and one for the 3D Euler equations — each with its Lean formalisation attached. The Euler result is the most significant. It specifies a family of initial conditions from which the equations evolve toward infinite velocity in finite time, with the smooth forcing maintained throughout. The Lean checker completed successfully on all three.

What they found

The 3D incompressible Euler equations — with smooth external forcing — can blow up in finite time. This is the first machine-verified proof of this result for these equations. Separately, OpenAI posted a claim that its system proved blowup for the full Navier-Stokes problem (with viscosity added), which is the literal Millennium Prize statement. Mathematicians are reading OpenAI's claimed proof now; the technical review will take weeks or months.

The two groups dispute priority. Buckmaster and Alpöge say they were not in contact with OpenAI before the concurrent announcements. OpenAI reportedly heard rumours of the mathematicians' work and accelerated its release. Whether either group influenced the other, and which result stands as the more significant contribution, is unresolved.

The catch

Buckmaster and Alpöge's result covers the Euler equations with smooth forcing — not the pure, unforced Euler equations, and not Navier-Stokes. The Millennium Prize asks specifically about unforced, viscous Navier-Stokes, where viscosity acts as a damper against singularities. Blowup in Euler with forcing does not automatically imply blowup in Navier-Stokes. OpenAI's claim addresses this gap — but has not been verified. A proof of a Millennium Prize Problem that is AI-generated and not yet independently checked by human experts carries genuine uncertainty, however large the compute behind it.

If you remember one thing: Two teams, within 24 hours, produced AI-assisted proofs for one of the hardest open questions in the mathematics of physical reality — one machine-verified for a related system, one claimed but unverified for the full prize problem — and neither story is over.

Why it matters

The equations governing fluid motion are not a niche corner of mathematics — they underlie weather forecasting, aircraft design, drug delivery, ocean modelling, and nuclear engineering. Whether those equations are mathematically complete, or whether they contain hidden singularities waiting to emerge, is a question with real consequences for how much we can trust computational models of physical processes. For ninety years, it remained open. This week produced two claims of progress, both AI-assisted, both involving the same underlying mathematical structure, both arriving so quickly that the field is still catching up. The more important point may be this: a form of reasoning that was the exclusive domain of the best human mathematicians — finding new ground in hard analysis — is now demonstrably something AI systems can contribute to, and in some formulations, dominate. The result is not yet at the level of "AI solved it alone." The humans set direction, chose targets, and verified the proof with Lean. But the intellectual labour — identifying structure in prior work, extending arguments, drafting the prose — was done by the models. The next decade of science may be shaped less by what models replace than by what they make possible for the researchers who use them well.

Decoder

Word of the day
Finite-time blowup
When the equations describing a physical system produce an infinite value — a singularity — after a finite amount of time. In fluid dynamics, it would mean a model predicts infinite velocity or pressure in a bounded window. For ninety years, nobody knew whether the Navier-Stokes and Euler equations could do this. This week produced two competing claims that at least a version of them can.
Lean
A proof-assistant program that checks mathematical arguments step by step against the axioms of logic. Unlike peer review (which relies on human readers), Lean cannot miss a hidden gap. A Lean-verified proof is as close to mathematically certain as the field knows how to get.
Euler equations
The 18th-century model of ideal, frictionless fluid flow — what Buckmaster and Alpöge proved blowup for. Simpler than Navier-Stokes because they omit viscosity, the internal resistance that makes fluids thick or thin.
Navier-Stokes equations
The more complete fluid model, adding viscosity. The Millennium Prize asks whether their smooth solutions can blow up. Viscosity damps singularities, so this is harder than the Euler case — and what OpenAI claims to have resolved.
Millennium Prize Problems
Seven mathematical questions identified by the Clay Mathematics Institute in 2000 as the most important unsolved problems in mathematics, each carrying a $1 million prize. Only one — the Poincaré conjecture — has been solved before this week's claims.
Chain-of-thought monitoring
Reading a model's visible step-by-step reasoning to check whether it is behaving safely. GPT-6 Astra's system card this week reported a "substantial decrease" in how reliably that reasoning can be monitored — meaning the model can reach conclusions without leaving a legible trail for safety researchers to inspect.
