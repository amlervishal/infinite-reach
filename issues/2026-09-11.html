Almost Right

The head of America's biggest startup accelerator told Washington this morning to leave the model-copying dispute alone. Meanwhile, a team of physicists at the University of Texas found something unexpected inside the reasoning traces of today's best language models: fractals.

The one thing

On the same day US security agencies were threatening action against Chinese companies for distilling American frontier models (covered here yesterday), Y Combinator's Garry Tan took the opposite view. Speaking at YC's Demo Day, Tan said the government should "do nothing" — arguing that the frontier labs built their own models on data they didn't own, and that restricting model access in the name of stopping copying would hurt the startup ecosystem far more than it would slow a determined competitor. It's the sharpest public counterpoint to the distillation alarm so far, and it lands from someone whose portfolio companies depend on cheap model access. The debate is no longer just about national security; it's about who gets to decide the price of intelligence.

Paper, explained: when reasoning models produce fractals

Fractal basins trap latent reasoning · William Gilpin et al. · University of Texas at Austin · arXiv 2609.04963

A team of physicists at UT Austin posted a paper this week that reframes how we understand AI reasoning — not as a careful, deliberate chain of logic, but as something closer to the turbulent motion of a pendulum: predictable on average, but sensitive in a way that can produce wildly different behaviour from nearly-identical starting points. The key finding is that reasoning models produce fractals.

The problem, and why it's hard

If you've ever noticed that asking a model to solve a hard problem can take ten seconds one day and two minutes the next — for what seems like the same kind of question — you've brushed against the puzzle this paper addresses. Reasoning models (the kind that "think out loud" before answering) are known to reason longer on harder tasks. But why some problems produce short reasoning traces and others produce enormous, meandering ones was unclear. The standard explanation — "harder problems need more thinking" — doesn't explain why two nearly-identical prompts can produce reasoning traces that differ by a factor of ten in length and cost. Something else is going on.

How it works

The researchers borrowed a concept from physics: transient chaos. Think of a ball rolling around on a lumpy landscape with several valleys. Each valley is a possible answer. Most of the time the ball rolls into a valley and stays there — it's settled. But some landscapes have ridges between the valleys: saddle points, places where the ground slopes toward a valley in one direction but away from it in another. A ball landing near a saddle point doesn't fall cleanly into any valley; it rolls around uncertainly, nearly stabilising, then sliding off toward a different one, then another. It eventually settles, but the path it takes is exquisitely sensitive to exactly where it started. Move the starting position by a millimetre and you get a completely different journey — a completely different length of wandering — even though both balls end up in the same valley.

That, the paper argues, is what reasoning models do when they face hard problems. The "landscape" is the model's internal state space — an abstract, high-dimensional space of possible thoughts. The "valleys" are correct answers. The "saddle points" are nearly-correct answers: solutions that are plausible enough to attract the model's reasoning for a while but not quite stable enough to hold it. A model reasoning about a hard Sudoku puzzle or a multi-step maths problem doesn't glide smoothly to the answer; it orbits these nearly-correct states — "almost right" — for extended periods before escaping toward the true solution. The trajectories through this landscape, when you map them out, form fractal patterns: self-similar shapes at every scale, the telltale signature of transient chaos.

The harder the problem, the more saddle points exist, and the more time the model spends trapped near them. That's why hard problems cost more: not because the model is doing more deliberate work, but because the problem's structure itself creates a more fractal, chaotic landscape to traverse.

What they actually did

Gilpin's team tested several leading reasoning models — including recurrent depth architectures — on four task types: Sudoku and maze-solving (for spatial reasoning), visual puzzles, and mathematical logic problems. For each task, they varied the difficulty and measured the resulting reasoning traces. They then applied a standard tool from dynamical systems theory — measuring the "fractal dimension" of the trajectories in the model's latent space — to quantify how fractal-like the reasoning paths were. They also ran the same prompts with tiny, controlled variations to measure how sensitive the paths were to initial conditions.

What they found

Across all four task types and all models tested, the fractal dimension of reasoning trajectories increased with task difficulty. Easy problems produced smooth, low-dimensional paths; hard problems produced high-dimensional, fractal-like ones. The same prompts, varied by a single word or even a single character, could produce reasoning traces that diverged sharply in length — sometimes by ten times or more. And crucially, in cases where the model reached the correct answer early but kept reasoning (a failure mode called "overthinking"), the team found that the model had often been pulled away from a correct stable state by a nearby saddle point — a nearly-correct answer that was just attractive enough to derail the final step.

One result the team flagged as particularly striking: on ARC-AGI (a famous visual-reasoning benchmark designed to test general intelligence), the fractal patterns were most intense in exactly the tasks where humans find it easy but models find it hard — suggesting the tasks are hard precisely because they have the most complex attractor landscape, not because they require the most computation.

The catch

This is a structural observation, not a fix. The paper explains why the behaviour happens but doesn't offer a way to make models reliably find the short path. It also focuses on recurrent-depth models — architectures that loop internally — and it's not yet proven that standard transformer-based reasoning models follow the same dynamics in exactly the same way, though the team found qualitatively similar fractal signatures in those too. The paper also can't tell you, for any given problem, in advance how expensive it will be — only that cost grows as a function of the problem's attractor complexity.

If you remember one thing: When a reasoning model seems to be overthinking, it isn't wasting effort — it's physically trapped in a nearly-correct state, orbiting an almost-answer until the mathematics of the problem's landscape lets it escape.

Why it matters

The fractal basins paper is the kind of research that lands quietly but could change how we build things. If reasoning cost is determined by the landscape of the problem — not just its surface difficulty — then the path to cheaper, more reliable reasoning isn't only about making models bigger or smarter. It's about understanding the shape of the problems we give them. That means better benchmarks, better prompt design, and potentially new training techniques that flatten the landscape rather than speeding up traversal. Meanwhile, the Garry Tan moment signals that the distillation debate is entering its political phase: Washington wants to restrict access, startups need it cheap, and every AI-adjacent business is choosing a side. The story of who controls the price of inference is only getting started.

Decoder

Word of the day
Transient chaos
A system behaves chaotically — sensitive to tiny starting differences — for a while, then settles into a stable state. Think of a pinball bouncing unpredictably before finally dropping into a hole. Today's paper argues that reasoning models do this every time they work through a hard problem.
Fractal basin
The set of starting points that lead to a given outcome. If you could colour-code every starting position by where it ends up, the boundaries between colours form a fractal — endlessly complex, never clean. That boundary is where the sensitive, expensive reasoning happens.
Saddle point
In a landscape of hills and valleys, a saddle point is a spot that dips in one direction but rises in another — like the centre of a horse saddle. In this paper, saddle points are nearly-correct answers: attractive enough to draw in a reasoning model, but not stable enough to hold it.
Distillation
Training a smaller or newer model by feeding it the outputs of a larger, more capable one. Like learning to cook by tasting a chef's food rather than reading cookbooks. The shortcut that US agencies say Chinese companies used to copy frontier models without building them from scratch.
MoE (Mixture of Experts)
A model architecture where only a fraction of the model activates for any given input — like a company where each employee specialises, and most stay at their desks while a few handle each customer. Cohere's North Small Translate has 218 billion total parameters but only 25 billion active at once.
FrontierMath
A benchmark of very hard mathematics problems written by professional mathematicians, designed to resist saturation — the state where every AI model aces every test. GPT-6 Astra scored 97.6% on the hardest tier this week; the benchmark's creators say it's effectively retired.
