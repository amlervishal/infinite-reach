Orthogonal

Four of the architects of Google DeepMind left the company last week to build something new — while a fresh paper explains why teaching an AI model many skills at once usually makes it worse at most of them, and suggests a fix borrowed from the branch of training that works by reward rather than correction.

The one thing

Jeff Dean, Sanjay Ghemawat, Oriol Vinyals, and Quoc Le resigned from Google last week to found Discovery Loop, an independent public-benefit corporation focused on automating the scientific process. Together, the four co-founders authored or co-authored papers that shaped the modern field: MapReduce, TensorFlow, sequence-to-sequence learning, AlphaGo. Their declared goal is to build AI that proposes its own experiments, runs them in parallel, learns from each result, and iterates without a human in the loop — starting with machine-learning research, then moving on to hardware design, drug discovery, and clean energy. Radical Ventures and Khosla Ventures are co-leading the seed round. Google serves as founding investor and cloud partner. Alphabet's stock dropped roughly 5% — an estimated $160–200 billion in market value — on the day the news broke. On the same day, Demis Hassabis stepped back from running DeepMind to become chairman of Google DeepMind and Alphabet's Chief Scientist, with Koray Kavukcuoglu moving up from CTO to senior vice president responsible for Gemini model development and research.

Paper, explained: when learning one thing breaks another

SFT Conflicts, RL Coexists: A Theoretical and Empirical Analysis of Multi-Task Learning for LLMs · Kejian Zhu, Zhuoran Jin, Shangqing Tu, Hongbang Yuan, Yushi Bai, Kang Liu, Juanzi Li & Jun Zhao (Tsinghua University / BAAI) · arXiv 2608.03573

It caught our eye because the problem it identifies is one you have almost certainly noticed in everyday AI use: a model that handles one kind of request beautifully and stumbles on something it should also be able to do. The paper offers the clearest explanation yet of why that happens — and points toward a fix.

The problem, and why it's hard

A large language model is built in stages. First comes pretraining: the model reads an enormous corpus and absorbs a general understanding of language, facts, and reasoning. Then comes fine-tuning, where you take that general model and improve it at specific skills: mathematics, coding, logical puzzles, scientific knowledge. The goal is a single model that is genuinely good at all of them.

The standard method for fine-tuning is supervised fine-tuning, or SFT: you show the model thousands of correct worked examples — here is a hard maths problem, here is the correct solution — and it adjusts its internal parameters to make those correct outputs more likely. This works well for one task at a time. The trouble, which this paper measures with unusual clarity, is what happens when you do it sequentially for several tasks. Train on mathematics first, then switch to coding: the model gets better at coding, but its maths score quietly drops. Train on four tasks in sequence and the model ends up worse at the first three than it was immediately after their dedicated sessions. The skills are fighting each other over the same internal parameters.

How it works

To understand why, imagine the model's millions of parameters as a vast landscape of dials. Every training step adjusts those dials in some direction to make the model better at the current example. If two tasks require turning the same dial in opposite directions, they fight. SFT updates are large and concentrated: a training step on maths turns a lot of dials hard toward maths — and those same dials happen to matter for coding and logic too, so the maths update quietly undoes earlier work. The skills compete for the same territory.

Reinforcement learning (RL) is different in one crucial respect. In RL, the model doesn't learn from correct examples; it learns from reward signals — indications of whether its output was good or bad — without ever seeing the "right" answer directly. That mechanism produces something mathematically surprising: the parameter adjustments for each task end up approximately orthogonal — pointing in directions that don't meaningfully cross. A training step toward better maths doesn't undo progress in coding, because they're travelling at roughly right angles through parameter space. The key technical reason is that RL uses advantage normalization: it rescales reward signals relative to a moving baseline, which bounds the gradient updates by their variance rather than their raw magnitude. Smaller, more diffuse nudges that spread into different directions rather than hammering a shared region.

Think of it this way: SFT is like painting different colour murals on the same wall in sequence — each coat partially covers the last. RL is more like painting in separate rooms that share no walls. Each piece of learning has space that is genuinely its own.

What they actually did

The team ran structured experiments across four domains: mathematics (tested on MATH500 and AIME 2025), science (MMLU subjects and GPQA-Diamond), coding (LiveCodeBench), and logic (Knights-and-Knaves puzzles). They trained models using SFT and RL separately on each domain, then measured cross-domain effects — what happens to the maths score when you fine-tune on coding? — and examined the gradient update patterns directly, mapping exactly where each training step pointed in parameter space. They also provided a theoretical proof showing why RL advantage normalization imposes a variance bound on gradient updates across tasks, while SFT interference scales with the raw gradient norm.

What they found

SFT training on one task reliably damaged performance on the others. RL training on one task largely preserved or improved performance on the others. The gradient analysis confirmed the mechanism: SFT updates clustered and overlapped, RL updates spread and separated. Based on this, the authors propose Parallel-RL: train each task completely independently using RL, then merge the resulting weights into a single model. Early results show that Parallel-RL performs better across all four domains than either sequential SFT or standard multi-task RL.

The catch

The experiments test a specific range of model sizes and a particular set of tasks. Whether the orthogonality finding holds at the largest frontier scale, or across a much broader and messier set of capabilities, has not yet been established. Parallel-RL also multiplies the compute cost: you need separate training runs for each domain before merging, which is feasible for a well-resourced lab and harder for everyone else. And the theory is elegant but idealised — real gradient landscapes are messier than the proof assumes, and the "approximately orthogonal" finding is an empirical one that could weaken in conditions the paper doesn't test.

If you remember one thing: Reinforcement learning lets a model learn multiple skills at once without any of them clobbering the others — because each RL training update travels in a direction that doesn't collide with the rest.

Why it matters

The Discovery Loop departure and the SFT-vs-RL paper are about different things, but they share a shape. In the paper, the old approach looks efficient on the surface but creates hidden friction: skills conflict, progress in one direction costs progress in another, and the overall system underperforms. The proposed fix is to allow each thing to proceed independently — in its own direction — before combining at the end. In the broader landscape this week, something structurally similar is happening. Researchers who built foundational infrastructure inside a very large institution are leaving to pursue independent directions, with Google serving as investor rather than director. Whether Discovery Loop produces better science than its founders would have done inside Google is unknowable yet. What is clear is that the field is comfortable enough now with its core methods that its best people feel confident enough to go build their own.

Decoder

Word of the day
Orthogonal
In geometry, orthogonal means "at right angles" — two lines that don't interfere with each other because they're pointing in completely different directions. In machine learning, when researchers say RL gradient updates are "approximately orthogonal" across tasks, they mean each skill's training nudge goes in a direction that doesn't undo the others. It's the difference between painting different murals on the same wall in sequence (each coat covers the last) and painting them in separate rooms that share no walls.
SFT (supervised fine-tuning)
Teaching a model by showing it correct worked examples — "here is the question, here is the right answer." Effective for a single skill, but the large parameter updates can overwrite progress on other skills when applied in sequence.
RL (reinforcement learning)
Teaching a model through reward signals rather than correct answers: "that response was good" or "that was poor." The model explores and adjusts over many attempts. Produces smaller, more diffuse parameter updates than SFT.
Gradient
The mathematical signal that tells a model which direction to nudge its parameters on a given training step. If two tasks push gradients in opposite directions, they fight; if they're orthogonal, they don't interact.
Task conflict
When fine-tuning on one skill degrades the model's performance on another. The model has a fixed set of parameters, and concentrating them on one task can physically displace what made another task work.
Advantage normalization
A step inside RL training that rescales reward signals relative to a running baseline, keeping the resulting gradient updates small and bounded by their variance rather than their raw size. This is what makes RL updates approximately orthogonal across tasks.
