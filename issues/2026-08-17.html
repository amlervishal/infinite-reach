Maps of the Interior

Anthropic published its second risk report this week and found, on its own audit, a gap in its own safety monitoring — 133 million conversations that ran without the relevant classifier active. The same day, a team at Tsinghua posted a paper with a faster method for reading what's actually happening inside a language model. Two stories that, taken together, are really one: the race to know what you've built.

The one thing

Anthropic's August 14 risk report disclosed an internal model — call it Model 2 — that outperforms its already-released Mythos 5 on most tasks. Anthropic has no plans to release it yet, because the full set of safety tests isn't finished. More striking than the shelved model is the reason the company raised its misalignment risk rating from "very low" to "low": not because it observed a new frightening behavior, but because its confidence in its own testing fell. Auditors discovered that roughly 133 million contractor conversations — a year of feedback from 50,000 workers — had run without the bioweapons classifier active. No customer was affected and the gap is now fixed, but finding it prompted the company to ask: what else might be missing? That is an unusually honest question for a lab to put in a public document.

Paper, explained: reading a model's vocabulary from its geometry

"SAEVerbalizer: Generating Explanations for Sparse Autoencoder Features via Representation Verbalization" · Weihan Meng et al., Tsinghua University · arxiv 2608.13538 · posted 13 August 2026

Researchers trying to understand what a language model has learned can now install a kind of anatomy layer that breaks the model's signals into millions of small, specialized detectors. The bottleneck has always been the same: somebody has to label each detector. This paper finds a shortcut, and the shortcut is to stop watching the detectors and start reading their shape.

The problem, and why it's hard

A sparse autoencoder (SAE) is a tool that interprets what's happening inside a language model. Imagine the model's hidden representation at any moment as a single point in a vast, high-dimensional space. The SAE learns to translate that point into a long list of small signals — most of them zero — where each non-zero signal corresponds to one narrow concept. One signal fires for "legal terminology." Another fires for "smells like citrus." Another for "mentions a specific person." At large scale, an SAE might have a million such signals, called features. Researchers call this decomposing the model's dense, inscrutable activity into an interpretable vocabulary.

The catch is that the vocabulary has no words. Every feature is mathematically defined — it exists as a direction in the model's vector space — but it has no English label. To label one feature, the standard approach collects a few hundred text passages that make that feature activate, hands them to another large model, and asks it to summarize what they share. Run this for a million features and the cost in time and compute is enormous. Worse, this behavioral approach can be shallow: it describes when a feature fires, not what it encodes in geometric terms. A feature might activate on "lavender" in English, "lavande" in French, and "lavendel" in German — and the behavioral summary might say "mentions purple flowers" while missing that the feature is, at its root, about a specific olfactory quality across languages.

How it works

SAEVerbalizer's insight is that every feature already has its definition: the decoder direction, a specific vector that represents what the feature encodes in the model's geometry. Instead of collecting behavioral evidence, the method injects that vector directly into the model's own representations and asks the model to translate it into words.

Here is the analogy to carry through. Imagine you are a wine sommelier and someone needs to know what a particular grape tastes like. The old method: pour a hundred glasses of wine that contain this grape, have a taster describe each one, then summarize the descriptions. The new method: extract the molecule that gives this grape its flavor, put it directly on the taster's tongue, and ask them to name it. You skip all the intermediate steps. The taster's palate is already trained — you just feed it the thing itself.

In the paper's framework, the "taster" is the language model itself. The "molecule" is the decoder direction. The researchers fine-tuned the model's downstream layers (the parts that turn internal states into text) on a dataset of (decoder direction, correct label) pairs. After training, you can take any decoder direction — even one the model has never seen — inject it at a middle layer, and let the fine-tuned downstream layers produce a description. The geometry of the injected direction guides the output just as the actual flavour molecule would guide the sommelier's tongue.

What they actually did

The team used Gemma 2 9B as their base model and the GemmaScope SAE dictionary — a set of sparse autoencoders Anthropic and Google previously released for interpretability research. They injected features at layer 20 of the 42-layer model, which sits in the middle depth where representations are rich but not yet fully committed to output tokens. They trained the downstream verbalization head and evaluated it on two standard interpretability benchmarks: detection score (does the generated label correctly predict which texts activate the feature?) and a human preference study comparing verbalization-generated labels against the slow example-based baseline. They also ran transfer experiments, asking whether the verbalization head trained on one SAE dictionary could label a completely different SAE dictionary — and whether it could, with a lightweight adapter, label features from a different model entirely.

What they found

On detection score, SAEVerbalizer matched or exceeded the example-based approach — meaning its labels were at least as useful for predicting when a feature activates. Human evaluators preferred the verbalization-based descriptions for specificity and accuracy. The cross-dictionary transfer worked: the method generalized to unseen SAE features without retraining. With the adapter, it extended to a second model architecture. The speed gain is substantial: the geometry is right there in the weights; you don't need thousands of forward passes to collect examples. The main failure mode is at the tail — rare features, those that activate on highly specific or unusual concepts, sometimes receive labels that are grammatically fine but oddly precise or weirdly worded, because the training data had few examples of how to translate that particular geometric region into natural language.

The catch

Everything here was tested on Gemma 2 9B. It is not yet known whether layer-20 injection is the right depth for larger models, or whether the approach holds at the scale of the models labs actually care about most — those with hundreds of billions of parameters. There is also a circularity in the evaluation: "does the label predict activation?" tests the label by the same behavioral criterion the old method optimizes for. If the old method's labels are systematically wrong about what a feature geometrically represents, the evaluation metric wouldn't catch that, and neither would SAEVerbalizer.

If you remember one thing: Instead of watching a feature to describe it, SAEVerbalizer reads its geometric shape — and that shape turns out to be a better description than the behavior.

Why it matters

Anthropic's risk report is careful to distinguish between two things: a bad outcome and reduced confidence that you'd catch one. The company didn't find a misaligned model. It found a monitoring gap — a year of contractor conversations that were never checked by the relevant classifier. What changed wasn't the underlying risk; what changed was the lab's honest accounting of how much it can see. That is a meaningful distinction, and it takes unusual intellectual honesty to raise a risk rating on that basis alone. The race now is to build the tools that let labs see more. SAEVerbalizer is one small piece of that: a faster way to label the learned vocabulary inside a model. A million features with accurate labels is a map. A million unlabeled detectors is still a fog.

Decoder

Word of the day
Decoder direction
The geometric fingerprint of a single learned concept inside a neural network — a specific direction in the model's multi-dimensional space that defines what a feature represents. Like a fingerprint that uniquely identifies one smell in a perfumer's vocabulary, before anyone has written the name for it.
Sparse autoencoder (SAE)
A tool installed over a language model's internals that decomposes its dense, mixed-up activations into thousands of narrow, single-concept detectors — most of them zero at any given moment. Interpretability researchers use SAEs to find what a model has learned.
Misalignment
When an AI system pursues goals that differ from what its makers intended. Anthropic now rates the risk of catastrophic harm from misalignment as "low" — up from "very low" in February. The change reflects reduced confidence in testing, not a new observed failure.
Responsible Scaling Policy (RSP)
Anthropic's self-imposed set of rules that defines capability thresholds at which the company must pause, run safety checks, and get approval before training or releasing a more powerful model. The August risk report covers progress against RSP commitments.
HRM (Hierarchical Reasoning Model)
An architecture for language models that structures reasoning in layers — roughly like outlining before writing. The design lets smaller models punch above their weight; DFM Mimir v1, this week's 1B-parameter standout, is built on this architecture.
GemmaScope
A set of pre-trained sparse autoencoders released by Google and Anthropic's researchers specifically for Gemma models — a kind of interpretability toolkit the SAEVerbalizer paper used as its test bed.
