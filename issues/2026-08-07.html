Written in the Work

China's most recognisable robot maker put a public price on itself yesterday. Meanwhile a paper from Zhejiang University asks whether the next breakthrough for AI agents isn't a bigger model at all — just a notebook of what worked last time.

The one thing

Unitree Robotics — the Hangzhou company that makes the dog-shaped quadrupeds and humanoid workers you have seen walking across factory floors in videos — priced its IPO on August 6 at 150.8 yuan per share, implying a valuation of 61 billion yuan, or roughly $9 billion. The offering raises about $904 million on Shanghai's technology-focused STAR Market, making Unitree mainland China's first publicly traded humanoid robot company. The numbers behind the listing are real: Unitree shipped more than 5,500 humanoid robots in 2025 — the most of any company in the world — and its cumulative quadruped sales topped 33,000 units, with exports making up over 40% of revenue. Gross margin on core product lines is 60%, and DeepSeek is among the strategic investors. Retail subscriptions open August 10. That creates a live comparison: Figure AI, the best-known US humanoid robotics startup, carries a $39 billion private valuation on near-zero disclosed revenue. When Unitree begins trading, the sector will have its first daily-priced reality check.

Paper, explained: agents that write their own recipe book

Memp: Exploring Agent Procedural Memory · Runnan Fang, Yuan Liang, Xiaobin Wang, Jialong Wu, et al. (Zhejiang University NLP Lab) · ACL 2026 Findings · arXiv 2508.06433

This paper asks a surprisingly simple question: what if an AI agent kept a notebook of everything it had learned how to do — and looked at it before starting a new job?

Why it caught our eye

Most research on making AI agents better focuses either on training a bigger model or on writing a better initial prompt. This paper tries a third option: give the agent a personal library of procedures it has distilled from its own past experience, and let it read from that library when the next task arrives. On a household-task benchmark, success rate nearly doubles with no change to the underlying model. That kind of gain from something so conceptually simple is worth paying attention to.

The problem, and why it's hard

When an AI agent tackles a task — say, navigate a simulated house and put the apple on the kitchen counter — it draws on two things: its general knowledge from training (what apples are, what kitchens contain, how to move objects) and any instructions given at that moment. What it doesn't have is procedural memory: the specific know-how that builds up when you've done similar tasks before. A human sous chef who has plated a hundred appetisers has muscle memory for the knife angles, the timing, the garnish order. An AI agent starting fresh on a new task has none of that accumulated habit — it's always the first day on the job.

The closest existing approach is fine-tuning: retraining the model on transcripts of past successful runs. That works, but it's expensive, slow, and once done, the model's "memory" is frozen. If the environment changes, you can't just update the recipe — you'd have to retrain again. What the field has been missing is something updatable and inspectable: a memory the agent can read, write, and revise.

How it works

The analogy that makes this click is a commis chef's recipe notebook. When you start a new kitchen, you bring a notebook filled with procedures you've worked out: the steps for making beurre blanc, when to add the cream, what the sauce looks like when it's about to break. Before service, you consult the relevant page. After service, you annotate: "add the cold butter more slowly," "use this pan not that one." The notebook improves with each shift without you having to rethink everything from scratch.

Memp works exactly this way, through a three-stage cycle:

Build. After each task attempt, the agent — or a separate "builder" model — reads the full trajectory (every action taken, every observation received, the final result) and distils two kinds of memory from it. The first is a fine-grained recipe: a specific, step-by-step account of what to do in situations like this. The second is a higher-level script: a more abstract outline ("in this class of task, first establish the starting state, then work backwards from the goal"). Both get stored in a memory library alongside a short description of the task they came from.

Retrieve. When a new task arrives, the agent encodes it as a text vector — essentially a numerical fingerprint of the task description — and compares it by cosine similarity to the fingerprints of every stored memory. The most similar memories get pulled and prepended to the agent's context. The agent now has a working brief that says: "Here is a task like yours, here is what worked, here is the step-by-step, here is the script."

Update. After the new task completes, the builder revisits the memories that were used and applies feedback from the actual result: adding steps that proved important, deleting steps that were wrong, and flagging memories that led to failures as low-confidence. Memory that proves reliable gets reinforced. Memory that keeps leading to mistakes gets corrected or retired.

The whole thing runs on top of the model's existing context window — no weight changes, no retraining, no external database beyond a text file of stored procedures.

What they actually did

The team tested Memp on two benchmarks using GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, and Qwen2.5 as the underlying models.

The first benchmark is ALFWorld, a standard testbed in which an agent must navigate a text-described household environment and complete chores — finding objects, picking them up, and placing them correctly. It is hard enough that agents without any procedural help struggle to get above 50% success. The second is TravelPlanner, a planning benchmark where the agent must design a multi-day itinerary satisfying a set of hard constraints (budget, transport, preferences) given only a city database and a user request.

What they found

On ALFWorld, Memp cut the average number of steps needed to complete a task from 23.76 to 15.01 — a 37% reduction in wasted action — while raising the overall success rate from 42.14% to 77.86%. On TravelPlanner, success rates climbed steadily as the memory repository grew denser with experience. The improvement was not front-loaded: early tasks don't benefit much because the library is sparse, but by the fifth or sixth batch of similar tasks, the gains are substantial and compounding.

The most surprising result was a transfer experiment. The team built a memory library using GPT-4o — a strong model — then migrated that library to a weaker model and asked the weaker model to use it. The weaker model's performance jumped significantly. The stored procedural know-how, in other words, is useful independent of who generated it. A small deployed model can benefit from the distilled experience of a larger one it never ran alongside.

Failure modes are worth naming: the Retrieve step is sensitive to surface wording. Two tasks that are procedurally identical but described differently — "go to the desk" versus "walk to the writing surface" — may not retrieve the same memories, leaving the agent without the relevant procedure. And the Update step can propagate errors if a mistake is made on an early task and the agent hasn't yet seen enough evidence to correct the wrong memory.

The catch

Both benchmarks are closed, well-structured domains: the house has known rooms, the travel database has known constraints. In those settings, procedural patterns repeat reliably and the memory library gets useful fast. Real-world agentic tasks are messier: if every task an agent gets is genuinely different from the last, the memory library never achieves the density where retrieval helps. The paper doesn't test on open-ended tasks, or in settings where tasks deliberately shift. It also doesn't address the question of what happens when the environment itself changes — if the layout of the house were updated, some stored memories might become actively harmful rather than helpful.

If you remember one thing: Giving an AI agent a notebook of its own past procedures — and letting it update that notebook after every job — roughly doubles its success rate at household tasks, with no change to the underlying model, because the second time you do something is always easier than the first.

Why it matters

Two things happened this week that belong in the same frame. A Chinese robot company went public with hard, audited numbers: real products, real margins, real shipments. And a university lab published a paper showing that AI agents can get dramatically better at tasks simply by remembering what they did before — no new model, no retraining, just a notebook. Together they point at the same underlying development: the question of whether any of this is actually useful is being answered in the world, not on a benchmark.

The Unitree IPO matters not because of the valuation but because of the reality check it creates. Public markets require revenue, audited financials, and quarterly accountability. When Unitree shares trade, the gap between "humanoid robots are coming" and "humanoid robots are here, making money, selling to factories" becomes legible as a number. That's useful even for people who will never buy the stock. The Memp result is quieter but structurally similar: it's easy to claim that AI agents will soon do complex multi-step work better than humans. It's more interesting to see that a modest, inspectable change — letting agents keep notes on their own jobs — gets them from 42% to 78% success on a standardised task. Progress at this layer, the tooling and memory layer rather than the model layer, is where a lot of the practical gains are coming from.

Decoder

Word of the day
Procedural memory
The kind of memory that stores how to do something, not what something is. Riding a bike is procedural memory. Knowing that bikes have two wheels is declarative memory. Large language models have huge declarative memory from training but limited procedural memory — they know about tasks but don't retain the experience of having done them before. The Memp paper proposes a way to give them the equivalent of a skill notebook.
Trajectory
In agent research, the full record of a task run: every action the agent took, every observation it received, and the final result. Memp distils these into reusable procedural memories.
Cosine similarity
A way of measuring how similar two pieces of text are by converting them to number-vectors and measuring the angle between them. A small angle (near-zero) means similar content; a large angle means different. Memp uses it to find stored memories that match a new task.
Fine-tuning
Continuing to train a language model on a new, smaller dataset after its main training is done — updating the model's weights directly. Unlike Memp's approach, fine-tuned memory is baked in and can't be inspected or corrected without retraining again.
MoE (Mixture of Experts)
An architecture where a model is really a collection of smaller specialist sub-networks, with a "router" directing each input to only a few of them. Qwen3.8-Max has 2.4 trillion parameters total but activates only ~95 billion for any given token — making it faster and cheaper to run than its size implies.
STAR Market
Shanghai's technology-focused stock exchange, launched in 2019 to give Chinese tech and science companies a domestic listing venue similar to the US NASDAQ. Unitree's listing there on August 10 is the first for a humanoid robotics company on China's A-share market.
