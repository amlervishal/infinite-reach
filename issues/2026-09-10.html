The Quiet Transfer

Three US intelligence and security agencies named six Chinese AI companies this week, accusing them of systematically copying American frontier models through billions of carefully crafted API queries. The technique has a name — distillation — and stopping it turns out to be harder than it looks. Meanwhile, Anthropic delayed its October listing and locked in a credit line six times larger than last year's, and DeepSeek quietly opened a beta for its fastest model yet.

The one thing

On September 8, the NSA, CISA, and FBI issued a joint advisory naming six Chinese AI companies — DeepSeek, Moonshot AI, Alibaba, MiniMax, StepFun, and Z.AI — for what they called "aggressive, malicious, and targeted" distillation of US frontier models since late 2024. The advisory says these companies drew billions of tokens across millions of queries from Claude, GPT, Gemini, and Grok, often through fraudulent accounts, bulk subscriptions, and proxy-routing services designed to evade detection. DeepSeek alone is accused of harvesting training data from multiple versions of Claude, GPT, and Gemini to build its R1 and V3 models. The recommended response is itself unusual: US agencies told AI providers not to simply ban suspected accounts, but to quietly degrade the responses they receive — feeding them subtly worse answers so they train inferior student models, without revealing that anything is wrong.

Paper, explained: when the defense creates a false sense of security

DistillGuard: Evaluating Defenses Against LLM Knowledge Distillation · Bo Jiang et al. · arXiv:2603.07835

The NSA advisory landed this week saying six Chinese AI companies had copied American frontier models through billions of API queries. What it didn't say is that the AI industry has been quietly building defenses against exactly this kind of attack — and that those defenses mostly don't work. A paper published in March set out to measure how badly they fail, and what it found is both useful and uncomfortable.

The problem, and why it's hard

To understand the attack, you first have to understand a legitimate technique called knowledge distillation. AI labs use it all the time. Here's the idea: you have a massive, expensive model — call it the teacher — and you want a smaller, cheaper model that behaves similarly. You don't need to retrain from scratch. You just let the teacher answer a huge number of questions, then train the small model on those question-answer pairs. The student learns to mimic the teacher's outputs, and ends up surprisingly close to matching it on many tasks. It's how labs compress a trillion-parameter model into one a phone can run.

The problem is that this same technique, pointed at a competitor's API, is a form of industrial espionage. An adversary with access to a frontier model through a paid API can run millions of queries, collect every response, and train their own model on the results. The teacher's training cost hundreds of millions of dollars in compute. The distillation campaign costs a fraction of that in API fees. If the teacher is GPT-6 Astra and the student is your own system, you absorb enormous amounts of expensive capability for relatively little money. This is what the NSA advisory describes happening, at scale, systematically, for at least two years.

How it works — and how providers try to fight back

AI providers have a few tools. The most-studied family is called output perturbation: slightly scramble the model's answers so they're still useful to legitimate users, but mislead any student model trying to learn from them. Think of it like a master chef who posts recipes online but always includes one subtle wrong step — just enough to ruin a copy, not enough to bother anyone cooking it for real.

There are other approaches too. Data poisoning plants bad examples in the teacher's outputs specifically for distillation training runs. Information throttling limits how much the API will return per query, making it more expensive and time-consuming to build up a training dataset. Each has a different cost, a different failure mode, and a different profile of who it hurts.

DistillGuard — a paper from Bo Jiang and colleagues published in March — is the first systematic attempt to evaluate all of these together, using a single controlled setup. The authors set up Qwen3-14B as the teacher and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct as the student, then ran nine different defense configurations against three benchmarks: MATH-500 (mathematical reasoning), HumanEval+ (code generation), and MT-Bench (general conversation and instruction-following).

What they actually found

The results are blunt: most output-level defenses are surprisingly ineffective against a naive attacker who doesn't even try to adapt. Paraphrasing-based perturbation — one of the most-deployed defenses, where the model slightly rewrites its own answers before returning them — barely degraded the student's performance. The student could still extract the semantic content; slightly different words don't confuse a neural network trained to absorb meaning rather than match text exactly.

The defenses that do work tend to do so by making the outputs noticeably worse. Aggressive throttling — cutting response length or detail — hurts distillation campaigns, but it also frustrates legitimate users. Heavy output scrambling degrades the student, but also degrades the usefulness of the API. The authors found a tricky and uncomfortable tradeoff: the more robustly you block distillation, the more you degrade the service for everyone. There's no free lunch where you can protect the model's capabilities without making them worse to access.

The paper also notes that the nine configurations it tested assume a "naive" attacker — one who takes the API's output at face value and doesn't try to compensate. A more sophisticated adversary could run many queries and average out the perturbation noise, use another model to denoise the outputs before training, or adapt the training procedure to be robust to scrambled labels. Real campaigns, like those described in the NSA advisory, are almost certainly more sophisticated than the naive baseline.

The catch

DistillGuard explicitly acknowledges that it hasn't tested against adaptive attackers — those who know a defense is in place and tune their attack to defeat it. That's not a bug in the paper; characterizing adaptive attacks is a separate, harder problem. But it means the headline findings ("defenses are weak") are an optimistic lower bound. Against a real adversary — say, a state-affiliated lab running industrial-scale campaigns — current defenses likely perform worse than what the paper measured.

There's also a deeper structural problem. The NSA advisory's own recommended response — quietly degrading accounts you suspect are conducting distillation — is itself a form of the information-throttling defense DistillGuard evaluated. The advisory is essentially asking providers to do something the research says is at best a partial solution, against adversaries who are almost certainly sophisticated enough to detect and route around it.

If you remember one thing: The best defenses against model-copying mostly hurt legitimate users without stopping a determined adversary — which is why the government's recommended response is covert sabotage, not a technical solution.

Why it matters

The NSA advisory makes something explicit that the AI industry has mostly danced around: the open-API model of frontier AI — where anyone with a credit card can query the best models in the world — has been systematically exploited to copy those models at industrial scale. Six companies, billions of queries, fraudulent accounts. This is not edge-case behavior; the advisory implies it's a routine commercial strategy.

The recommended defense — quietly degrading the outputs for suspected accounts — is revealing in its own right. It acknowledges that you cannot reliably tell a legitimate power user from a systematic extractor. You can't simply ban your way out of this. The research shows that technical defenses (scrambling outputs, throttling responses) mostly hurt your own users while only weakly deterring a sophisticated adversary. The US agencies are essentially recommending covert sabotage because they don't have a better option.

The deeper question is about the economics of AI progress. Distillation — legitimate or otherwise — is one reason capable models are proliferating so quickly: you don't need to fund an enormous training run if you can approximate a frontier model through API queries. That's good for access and competition; it's bad for the companies that spent billions building the original. Where the line between inspiration and theft falls in machine learning is one of the unresolved legal and ethical questions of this moment, and last week's advisory is a signal that governments are no longer willing to leave the answer to the market alone.

Decoder

Word of the day
Distillation
In AI, distillation means training a small, cheap model to imitate a large, expensive one — by having the small model learn from the big model's answers rather than from scratch. Think of it as the difference between studying with a textbook (original training) and getting a private tutor who has already read all the textbooks (distillation). You still have to do the learning, but the tutor does the heavy lifting of knowing what's important.
API
Application Programming Interface — the channel through which outside programs send requests to a service. For AI models, the API is how you send a question and get an answer back. Pay per query. This openness is what makes distillation attacks possible.
Output perturbation
A defense where a model slightly scrambles its own answers before returning them — still useful to a legitimate user, but designed to confuse a model trying to learn from those answers. The research says it mostly doesn't work well enough.
MoE (Mixture of Experts)
An architecture where a large model is actually many small specialist sub-networks. Each input gets routed to only a few specialists, so the model is huge in total but cheap to run per query. DeepSeek's V4.1 Flash (552B parameters, only 8–16B active at once) uses this approach.
Frontier model
A shorthand for an AI model at the current cutting edge of what's possible — roughly, the best models from Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, and a handful of others. "Frontier" because they're at the frontier of capability, not because they're necessarily available to everyone.
Revolving credit facility
A pre-arranged line of credit a company can draw from and repay repeatedly — like a credit card for corporations. Anthropic's new $15 billion facility means banks will lend it up to $15B on short notice, signaling confidence in the company's trajectory ahead of its IPO.
