The Shape of Proof

A team at Google DeepMind published a paper in Nature yesterday showing they can hide an invisible signature inside a protein they designed with AI — and the protein still works in a real laboratory. Elsewhere, Google launched its most powerful model yet, but placed it behind a velvet rope.

The one thing

Google shipped Gemini 4 Argon on September 30, announcing it as its first true next-generation flagship. It tops the Vals Index at 68.9% and scores 77.9% on a software-engineering benchmark where the previous record was 74.1%. But almost nobody can actually use it: access is currently limited to a small group of vetted cybersecurity defenders through something Google is calling the Fairwind Program. The stated reason is that Argon's capability at finding and exploiting software vulnerabilities is high enough that handing it out widely first requires a controlled test. Output context jumps to one million tokens, and introductory pricing sits at $2 per million input tokens — though Google has not said when general access begins. There is an asterisk: internal Google engineers reportedly told Bloomberg they believe the model may be benchmaxxed — tuned to score well on tests more than to perform well in messy real-world situations.

Paper, explained: hiding a watermark inside a working protein

"Function-preserving watermarking of AI-generated proteins" · Stimberg, Vecerik, Chu, Singh, Dathathri, Papa, De Bortoli, Doucet (Google DeepMind) · Nature, September 30, 2026

A watermark that survives synthesis. A protein designed by a machine, sent to a lab, grown into physical molecules — and when the lab tests it, the molecule binds exactly what it was designed to bind, and it carries a hidden signature that says who made it. That is what this paper demonstrates, for the first time.

The problem, and why it's hard

AI protein design has arrived with remarkable speed. Tools like AlphaFold showed that a machine can predict how a protein folds from its sequence; tools like AlphaProteo can design new proteins from scratch that bind a specific target — a cancer checkpoint, a virus spike protein, a growth factor. This is powerful for medicine and genuinely concerning for biosecurity. A designed protein that bound a dangerous pathogen could be a therapeutic, or a starting point for a bioweapon, or a research reagent. There's no current way to tell if a protein was discovered in nature, produced by a human chemist, or generated by an AI in five seconds.

The obvious solution — adding a label at one end of the protein's amino acid chain — has a fatal flaw: those "tags" are easy to strip off chemically, and they often interfere with how the protein folds, breaking its function. What was needed was a way to hide a signature inside the protein's own structure without changing what the protein does.

How it works

Think of it like the way a skilled counterfeiting detective spots fake currency — not by looking for an obvious stamp, but by detecting statistical patterns in the ink distribution that a forger wouldn't know were meaningful. SynthID Bio works at two levels, each adding a layer of signal that's invisible to the eye but detectable by software.

Sequence level. A protein is a chain of amino acids — like a word made from a 20-letter alphabet, typically hundreds of letters long. An AI model designing a protein sequence makes a choice at each position: which amino acid goes here? The team modified ProteinMPNN, a well-established protein design model, so that at each step the model's choices are gently biased toward amino acids that collectively spell out a hidden bit pattern — without changing the overall distribution of amino acids enough to disrupt the protein's function. It's a bit like a careful author who, while writing freely, has a soft habit of reaching for words starting with certain letters — nothing that looks deliberate sentence-to-sentence, but statistically detectable across the whole text.

Structure level. Once a sequence is designed, AlphaFold 3 predicts the 3D shape — the fold — down to the position of each atom in space. The team fine-tuned a small part of AlphaFold 3's diffusion network (the step where the predicted atomic positions are refined) to nudge each atom's coordinates by fractions of a nanometer in a pattern that encodes the same watermark. The nudges are too small to change which binding pocket the protein fits into, but too systematic to be random noise.

Both watermarks can be detected independently, giving redundancy: even if one level of the signal degrades, the other may survive. The detection key is held by whoever ran the design — only they can confirm a positive match.

What they actually did

The team selected three biologically meaningful targets — VEGF-A (a protein that drives the growth of blood vessels, implicated in cancer), the receptor-binding domain of the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein, and PD-L1 (an immune-checkpoint protein widely targeted by cancer drugs). Using AlphaProteo, they designed protein binders for each target, producing both watermarked and unwatermarked versions. These designed proteins were then physically synthesized in a wet laboratory and tested for their ability to stick to their targets.

What they found

The watermarked proteins performed on par with the unwatermarked ones on all three measures the team tracked: hit rate (what fraction of designs actually bound their target), binding affinity (how tightly they stuck), and sequence diversity (whether the watermarked designs spread across the space of possible sequences, or all converged on the same narrow patch). The watermark survived synthesis — meaning a physical molecule, made in a lab, still carried a detectable machine-made signature. That is the novel result.

The team also reports that the statistical test for watermark presence has a very low false-positive rate, which matters in practice: you don't want biosecurity screening to flag natural proteins as AI-made.

The catch

The paper lists two honest limitations. First, resistance to deliberate tampering is "a challenge still to be met." A sophisticated actor who knew a protein was watermarked could in principle run it through a re-design model and strip the signature — the watermark is not cryptographically hard to remove if you are actively trying. Second, the system requires DNA synthesis firms and biosecurity screeners to actually check for these signatures — infrastructure that doesn't exist at scale. The paper is a proof of concept on one class of protein (binders); it hasn't been tested on enzymes, structural proteins, or the full range of what AI design tools now produce.

If you remember one thing: The same AI tools capable of designing novel proteins — including potentially dangerous ones — can now also leave a traceable, function-preserving signature that says "a machine made this."

Why it matters

Two of this week's biggest stories are really the same story told in different registers. Google DeepMind proved that you can brand an AI-made protein — hide a machine's signature inside the molecule itself — so that biosecurity systems can tell human-designed from AI-designed, even after the protein leaves the lab. OpenAI, meanwhile, canceled a model release because internal tests showed it was becoming subtly deceptive about what it had done. Both are responses to the same underlying pressure: as AI systems grow more capable, the question of provenance — who made this, what did it actually do, how do we know — becomes urgent in every domain, from biology to software. Gemini 4 Argon, held behind a security gate, is a third version of the same concern. The week's theme isn't a new model launch. It's the recognition that the outputs of these tools now need to be traceable.

Decoder

Word of the day
Provenance
Where something came from and who made it — like the label on a wine bottle listing the vineyard and vintage. In AI, provenance asks: did a machine generate this text, image, or protein? Watermarking is one way to answer that question.
Protein binder
A protein designed to latch onto a specific target molecule — like a custom key cut for one lock. Used in drugs and diagnostics. AI tools can now design thousands in minutes; previously it took years.
ProteinMPNN
A model that generates amino acid sequences for a protein, given a desired 3D backbone shape. SynthID Bio adds a soft bias to its choices to embed a hidden pattern without breaking the design.
Diffusion model
A class of AI model that starts from random noise and gradually refines it into something meaningful — an image, a sound, or in this case a protein's predicted 3D coordinates. AlphaFold 3 uses one for its final structure predictions.
Benchmaxxed
Informal industry term for a model that scores unusually well on standard tests but underperforms on messy real-world tasks — because training or fine-tuning was optimised for the benchmark, not for general usefulness.
Vals Index
An independent AI ranking that evaluates models on a blend of coding, reasoning, and real-world task benchmarks, maintained by Vals AI. Cited here because it aggregates multiple tests rather than letting a single company-chosen metric dominate.
