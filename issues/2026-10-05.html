The Appointed Hour

The United States named its first AI Czar on Sunday and started a 120-day countdown — the most formal step the US government has taken toward coordinating its relationship with the technology. Elsewhere, North Korea claimed a missile that adjusts its own flight path by algorithm, and a paper out of Beijing found that robots work better, and cost half as much to run, when you trust them to handle the routine steps on their own.

The one thing

On Sunday, President Trump signed an executive order creating a "Super Intelligence Force" — a White House task force on AI — and named Jay Clayton, the Director of National Intelligence, as its head, a title the order calls the "AI Czar." Clayton is joined by the FTC chairman, the head of the Office of Personnel Management, and the Pentagon's undersecretary for research. The group has 120 days to deliver a report on AI risks and opportunities and will act as the federal government's coordination point with AI companies, critical-infrastructure operators, and the public. This is not a regulatory body and has no enforcement powers — but it is the first time the US government has appointed a named individual to be responsible, across agencies, for the country's AI posture. The 120-day clock runs to early February.

Paper, explained: The robot that stopped asking so many questions

PyRUA-Lean: Reducing Unnecessary Actions in Robot Agent Planning · Si Ruiyang, Bi Jianxin, Yang Shunyu et al. · Peking University, NUS, NVIDIA · arXiv 2610.01939

Here is a counterintuitive result: if you make a robot ask an AI for help less often, the robot completes tasks more reliably — and costs half as much to run. That is the core finding of PyRUA-Lean, a paper from Peking University, the National University of Singapore, and NVIDIA submitted on October 1.

The problem, and why it's hard

Robot agents that can navigate a kitchen, stock a warehouse shelf, or assemble parts typically work by calling a large vision-language model (VLM) — a powerful AI that can look at a camera feed and decide what to do next — at nearly every step. See the object, ask the model: should I pick it up? It moves an inch. Ask again: what now? This approach works, but it has two serious problems. First, it is expensive: each model call costs money and takes time, and a task involving dozens of micro-actions can require dozens of calls. Second, flooding the model with trivial questions — "the cup is now 2 centimetres closer, what do I do?" — actually degrades the quality of its decisions on the steps that actually matter, because the model has no way to distinguish a meaningful moment from a routine one.

Researchers have tried to fix this by having the agent skip unnecessary queries, but deciding when to skip and when to ask is itself a hard problem. Skip too much and the robot gets lost; ask too often and you are back where you started.

How it works

Think of the difference between a surgeon who consults a senior colleague after every suture versus one who writes a procedure plan at the start and only calls for help when something unexpected happens — a bleed, a rupture, an anomaly. The second surgeon gets better outcomes because the senior colleague's attention is reserved for the moments that actually need it. PyRUA-Lean does the same thing for robots.

Instead of asking the VLM what to do at each step, the agent asks the VLM once to write a short block of Python code covering several steps — a mini-program like "pick up the red cup, carry it to the sink, put it down." That code runs directly on the robot. If everything goes according to plan, the VLM is never queried again. Only when the robot encounters a genuine exception — the cup fell, the shelf is in the wrong place, the task state has changed unexpectedly — does it pause, report back, and ask the model for an updated plan.

The code blocks are not hand-written rules. The VLM generates them on the fly, conditional on what it sees. The key insight is that language models are actually quite good at writing short, reliable sequences of robot-control code for common household and industrial tasks; what they are not good at is answering the same trivial question forty-nine times in a row without getting confused. Separating "write the plan" from "execute the plan" lets each capability do what it is suited for.

The authors call this approach "lean orchestration": the agent is still AI-driven at the planning level, but it trusts its own execution more, and escalates less. The name PyRUA-Lean reflects both the Python-based rule-action architecture and the lean philosophy — do more with fewer interruptions.

What they actually did

The team tested PyRUA-Lean against a standard baseline (per-step VLM querying) across 700 simulated task instances drawn from three benchmark suites: LIBERO-PRO (household object manipulation), RoboTwin 2.0 (bimanual robot coordination), and RoboCasa365 (a large-scale kitchen environment). Tasks ranged from setting a table to sorting objects by colour. They used GPT-6 Astra as the underlying VLM for most tests, with ablations across other models to confirm the approach is model-agnostic.

What they found

Overall task success went from 63.1% to 71.7% — a roughly 14% relative improvement. At the same time, the number of VLM or language-model calls per task dropped by 49%, and input tokens consumed fell by 65%. The cost per task dropped from $1.63 to $0.74 — less than half. The paper's own description calls this "a rare simultaneous win on accuracy and efficiency." The failure modes are mostly predictable: tasks with very unusual or ambiguous states — a counter covered in identical objects, a drawer that won't open — still tripped the system into over-querying or gave it code that broke. The improvement was largest on well-structured tasks with clear object identities and predictable physical states.

The catch

All 700 tests are in simulation, not physical hardware. Simulation is standard practice in robotics research and gives useful signal, but real-world environments have noise that simulators do not capture — slippery surfaces, varying lighting, imprecise motor control. The generated code blocks also only work as well as the VLM's physical-world understanding; a model that miscalibrates the size of an object will write code that fails silently. The paper does not test failure recovery: what happens when the exception-triggered re-plan itself produces bad code is not studied. And the cost numbers assume GPT-6 Astra pricing — change the model and the arithmetic changes too.

If you remember one thing: Asking an AI fewer, better questions turns out to produce better answers — a principle that applies well beyond robots.

Why it matters

Two things happened this week that look unrelated but tell the same story. The US government named an "AI Czar" and gave him 120 days to figure out what the country's AI policy should be; North Korea claimed a missile that navigates itself. Both are responses to the same underlying shift: autonomous AI systems are now capable enough that nations feel they need formal stances toward them, not just company policies. The 120-day deadline is a clock. Whether a report actually changes anything depends on what comes after it — which the executive order does not specify.

Meanwhile, PyRUA-Lean, Karpathy's ladder post, and Garry Tan's deleted markdown all point toward a second shift: the engineering culture around AI is maturing from "call the model constantly" to "know when not to call it." The early instinct — throw everything at the most powerful model, ask it every question, let it make every decision — turns out to be neither the cheapest nor the most effective approach. Letting a system handle its own routine work, and reserving the expensive intelligence for genuine decision points, is a pattern you will see everywhere over the next year.

Decoder

Word of the day
Czar
In US government usage, a "czar" is an informal term for a senior official appointed to coordinate policy across multiple agencies on a specific topic — like the "drug czar" or "cyber czar." They are not elected, have no statutory authority of their own, and cannot make law. Their power comes from proximity to the president and their ability to convene agencies that would otherwise not talk to each other.
VLM
Vision-language model. An AI that can process both images (or camera feeds) and text at the same time — letting a robot "see" its environment and receive natural-language instructions in one system.
Token
The basic unit an AI model processes. Roughly three-quarters of a word. When a paper says it used "65% fewer tokens," it means the AI had 65% less text to read and process — a direct proxy for cost and speed.
ASD-STE100
Simplified Technical English — a writing standard originally created by the aerospace industry (ASD = AeroSpace and Defence) for aircraft maintenance manuals. It limits vocabulary and sentence structure to reduce ambiguity. Karpathy's insight is that asking an AI to write in STE produces cleaner, less hedged output.
Benchmark
A standardised test used to compare AI systems. Like a driving test that every new car must pass — the same track, the same obstacles — so you can compare one model against another fairly. Scores only reflect performance on the specific test; real-world behaviour can differ.
CBRN
Chemical, Biological, Radiological, and Nuclear — the four categories of weapons of mass destruction. When companies say they restrict a model's access over "CBRN risks," they mean they are worried the model could provide information that helps someone build or deploy such weapons.
