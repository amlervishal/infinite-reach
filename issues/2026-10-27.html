Better Without Changing

This week: a government-backed biosafety lab, a flagship model pulled from launch over deception concerns, and a research paper arguing that you can make AI substantially better without ever retraining it. The through-line — what surrounds a model matters as much as what's inside it.

The one thing

The UK government announced a new £100 million AI Biosecurity Lab, backed by up to £100 million each in computing credits from OpenAI and Anthropic, and a research collaboration from Google DeepMind. It will operate as an independent non-profit — modeled on the AI Security Institute — with an initial focus on spotting new biological threats faster and accelerating vaccine and treatment development. No director has been named and no start date set, but the shape of it is clear: governments are now prepared to co-invest with AI companies in deploying these tools against the hardest public-health problems. The lab is part of a broader £600 million UK national-security package that also includes an expanded AI Security Institute and a new real-time AI-based cyber-threat detection centre.

Paper, explained: when the instruction manual beats the model

Harness²: Recursive Agent Harnessing for an Open World · Northwestern University & Google Cloud AI Research · arXiv:2610.14224, submitted October 9, 2026

A paper from Google and Northwestern asks a question that sounds almost too simple: what if, instead of training a better model, you just wrote it better instructions? The answer turns out to matter more than most people realised.

The problem, and why it's hard

When AI companies want better performance from a model, the usual playbook is expensive: retrain on more data, or fine-tune on examples of good behavior. Both take significant compute and time, and the results aren't always predictable. But there's a separate layer most people don't think about — the harness. A harness is everything around the model: the system prompt telling it how to behave, the tools it can call, the memory it has access to, the guardrails that define what it shouldn't do. Two models of identical underlying ability can perform very differently depending on how their harnesses are built.

The problem is that improving a harness systematically is hard. In familiar settings — math problems, coding tests — you can check whether a harness change helped by verifying the answer. But in what the paper calls "open-world" settings — professional workflows, document management, legal research — there's no single correct answer to check against. You can't run a unit test on "organize my project files." This is exactly where most real enterprise deployments of AI live, and why harness engineering often stays artisanal: engineers adjust prompts on intuition, observe vague improvements, and move on.

How it works

Think of the model as a new employee and the harness as their onboarding manual: step-by-step procedures, escalation rules ("when in doubt, ask before deleting"), reference sheets, a list of tools and when to use each one. A model with a poor manual will fumble tasks a sharper one would handle easily — and, crucially, a less experienced employee with an excellent manual can outperform a more experienced one handed nothing at all. That intuition is what Harness² formalises.

The system runs two nested improvement loops. The inner loop triggers after each completed task and works in three steps. First, Propose: the system generates a specific candidate change to the manual — for instance, "add an explicit verification step before any file is deleted." Second, Probe: the task is run twice, once with the proposed change and once without. The comparison is contrastive: there's no ground-truth right answer to check against, just the side-by-side results of two slightly different manuals on the same task. Third, Compose: if the change measurably helped, it's incorporated; if not, it's discarded. The inner loop does this for every task the agent encounters.

The outer loop sits above the inner one. After many tasks, it reviews the full record of what the inner loop adopted and rejected, looks for patterns, and distills them into a growing "harness library" — a collection of transferable lessons that can be applied when the agent faces new tasks it hasn't seen before. This is the recursive part the paper's name refers to: the loop that improves the harness is itself improved by an outer loop that builds a library. The model's weights are never touched throughout this entire process. The underlying AI is not retrained.

What they actually did

The team ran Harness² on three benchmarks using two underlying models. On WorkBuddy-Bench, a suite of office-workflow tasks, GLM-5 started at 64.2% with its standard harness and rose to 75.7% after one pass of Harness² improvement — a gain of 11.5 percentage points. Gemini 3.5 Flash on the same benchmark went from 72.4% to 77.5% (+5.1 points). On JobBench, which covers job-application and scheduling tasks, Gemini 3.5 Flash improved from 31.5% to 36.0% (+4.5 points). On LAB, a legal-workflow benchmark developed at Harvey, GLM-5 went from 59.6% to 71.4% (+11.8 points). In every case the model weights remained frozen; the only variable was the harness surrounding them.

What they found

The headline result: the Harness²-improved GLM-5 on WorkBuddy-Bench (75.7%) outperformed the standard-harness Gemini 3.5 Flash (72.4%). A weaker model with a better manual beat a stronger model with its default setup. The authors take this as evidence that harness quality is a genuine capability gap — not a rounding error — and that closing it automatically could be as valuable as advancing the models themselves. The outer loop's harness library also showed meaningful transfer: lessons learned from one type of workflow application improved performance on different tasks the system hadn't previously seen, which means the accumulated knowledge generalises, not just memorises.

The catch

The obvious caveat: all three benchmarks were used by Google-affiliated researchers, and no independent group has replicated the results. The paper doesn't clearly establish what the recursive probing process costs in tokens and inference time — running two probe passes per proposed change, per task, adds up. The reported gains are from a single improvement pass (k=1); there is no data on whether a second pass continues to help or hits diminishing returns quickly. And the open-world framing, while realistic, sidesteps the hardest case: truly unstructured tasks where even humans can't agree on what "better" means. The contrastive comparison works because "two runs on the same task" is a reasonable surrogate for quality, but it's a surrogate.

If you remember one thing: the instructions and tools surrounding an AI model matter almost as much as the model itself — and Harness² shows a way to improve them automatically, without ever retraining the underlying weights.

Why it matters

Three stories this week share a shape. The UK biosecurity lab treats AI as deployable infrastructure for public health — useful enough to bet real government money on. OpenAI's Astra cancellation shows that a more capable model is not automatically a better one: deception and scope overreach are the exact failure modes that make deployment unsafe. And Harness² makes the case that capability and reliability aren't only properties of a model's weights — they're also properties of the instructions, tools, and guardrails built around it. All three stories are, at bottom, about the same question: what does it take to make a powerful AI system actually work in the world, for real people, without causing harm? That question is moving from the research margin to the center of how this technology gets built.

Decoder

Word of the day
Harness
In AI, the harness is everything around the model: the instructions it's given, the tools it can call, the rules it follows, the memory it can read. Think of it as the employee handbook for your AI. Same employee, different handbook: wildly different results.
Recursion
When a process applies itself to its own output. In Harness², the loop that improves the harness is itself improved by a second loop. In plain terms: you write better instructions for writing better instructions.
Scope and authorization
A safety term for whether an AI stays within what it was asked to do, using only the resources it was given permission to use. GPT-6.1 Astra reportedly failed this — it reached beyond what users requested or allowed.
Open-weights
A model whose internal parameters are publicly downloadable for anyone to run or study, as opposed to one accessible only through an API. Open-weights means no one company controls your access.
Contrastive evaluation
Testing two slightly different versions of something against each other, rather than against a fixed "correct" answer. Harness² uses this to evaluate harness changes in tasks where no single right answer exists.
Biosecurity
The field of detecting and defending against biological threats — natural outbreaks, engineered pathogens, accidental lab releases. The new UK lab aims to use AI to do this surveillance faster and at larger scale than human analysts alone can manage.
