The Address You Never Moved To

Microsoft finishes pre-training the largest model its labs have ever built. At the same moment, a new paper names the quiet flaw in every AI assistant that builds a picture of you over time: it writes down what you said you might do, and quietly treats it as what you did.

The one thing

On Thursday, Microsoft AI announced it had finished pre-training MAI-Base-2 — a 2.8-trillion-parameter model trained on 50 trillion tokens across 16,000 of Nvidia's latest GB200 chips over 38 days. The design is a mixture-of-experts: only 80 billion of those 2.8 trillion parameters are active for any given request, which keeps running costs manageable while the total capacity sits far above any previous Microsoft model. CEO Mustafa Suleyman posted that post-training is now underway and results are coming soon. He also reportedly told staff privately that MAI-Base-2 outperforms the base model behind GPT 5.6 Sol on Microsoft's internal benchmarks — using a quarter of the training compute. That comparison hasn't been independently verified and Microsoft hasn't published the methodology. But if the efficiency claims hold, Microsoft has stopped being just an investor and distributor of OpenAI's work and become a genuine frontier lab in its own right. The weights are not yet public; Microsoft plans to license them through its Foundry platform to vetted partners.

Paper, explained: when your assistant treats your plans as your facts

"AgentMemGate: Addressing Speculation Contamination in Conversational Assistant Memory" · Sharma, Fowlersmith & Maamari · arXiv 2610.07707 · PALM Workshop, NeurIPS 2026 · source

Every AI assistant that "remembers" you is quietly facing a problem nobody had named until now. This paper names it — speculation contamination — and proposes a fix so clean you'll wonder why the field didn't build it in from the start.

The problem, and why it's hard

When you talk to an AI assistant across many sessions, it builds a picture of you: your job, your city, your routines, your preferences. It does this by extracting facts from what you say. That sounds straightforward, but people talk about the future constantly. "I'm thinking of switching jobs." "We might get a dog." "I'm seriously considering a move to Seattle for this new role." A well-designed memory layer needs to know which of these are settled facts and which are still possibilities. Most systems don't make that distinction. They store "I might move to Seattle" the same way they'd store "I live in Seattle." Weeks pass. The possibility becomes the permanent record. The plan you never carried out takes up residence in your assistant's understanding of you.

The authors — Chirag Sharma, Benjamin Fowlersmith, and Karime Maamari — call this speculation contamination. It sounds like a minor bug, but it quietly poisons the whole memory layer. An assistant that "knows" you live somewhere you don't, hold a job you never took, or own a dog you decided against will be subtly wrong in every conversation that touches those subjects. And because the memory is invisible to you, the errors compound silently.

How it works

The proposed fix is a gate that runs before anything is written to memory. Think of it like an archivist who checks every proposed new entry: is this a record of something that actually happened, or something the person was only considering? The gate — AgentMemGate — classifies each extracted statement into one of four buckets: a speculation (a hypothetical: "I might"), a completed event (something that happened: "I accepted the offer"), a correction (a revision of a previous fact: "Actually it's Portland, not Seattle"), or other (preferences, opinions, context).

Only the last three categories go into permanent memory. Speculations are diverted to a pending queue — a quarantine area. They wait there until the user's future messages either confirm the plan ("We did it — we're moving") or abandon it ("We decided to stay"). An unresolved plan stays pending indefinitely and never corrupts the memory the assistant actually relies on.

The analogy that makes it click: most memory systems work like a court reporter who transcribes everything, including "we might appeal this ruling." AgentMemGate is a court reporter who knows that an appeal is only a fact once it's filed. Until then, it belongs in the margin notes, not the case record.

What they actually did

The authors assembled a dataset of 147 multi-session conversations where hypothetical plans are later either confirmed, abandoned, or simply never resolved. They ran two popular open-source memory systems — Mem0 and Graphiti — on this dataset, measuring how often an unresolved speculation was stored as a settled fact. Then they layered AgentMemGate on top and measured again.

What they found

Without the gate, Mem0 stored unresolved hypotheticals as facts 35.2% of the time; Graphiti did so 27.3% of the time. With the most aggressive extraction style the researchers tested, contamination reached 87.5%. Task accuracy — the share of questions the assistant answered correctly when the answer depended on accurate memory — sat at 65%.

With the gate in place, contamination on the standard benchmark fell to zero. Task accuracy rose to 95%. On a harder, more natural-language held-out set, a small residue of contamination remained (3.4–5.7%), but task accuracy still climbed 9–13 percentage points. The gate didn't just patch the worst cases; it improved reliability across the board.

The catch

The gate works by matching extracted statements against structured profile fields — recognizable slots like "city," "occupation," or "pet." When a hypothetical is about something that doesn't map to a neat slot — "I'm thinking of learning Portuguese," "I might volunteer on weekends" — the gate may never see it, and contamination can pass through silently. The authors flag this as their main open problem: the gate is only as powerful as the schema it checks against.

If you remember one thing: Before an AI assistant writes something to its long-term memory about you, it should ask one simple question — has this actually happened yet? AgentMemGate is the first systematic attempt to build that question into the architecture rather than leaving it up to luck.

Why it matters

This week surfaced two problems that belong in the same thought. Microsoft's MAI-Base-2 marks the moment a company long known as an AI distributor completed a training run that puts it alongside the handful of labs that build frontier models from scratch. That matters for competition, for enterprise AI supply chains, and for the pace of capability growth — because now there are more parties capable of pushing the frontier, not just one or two. Meanwhile, AgentMemGate points at the reliability layer that will have to work before any of these models can be trusted with something as intimate as your personal context. You can have the most capable model in the world; if its memory confuses your plans with your facts, it will end up confidently wrong about your own life. Scale is one problem. Trust is a different one. This week, both showed up.

Decoder

Word of the day
Speculation contamination
When an AI assistant that keeps notes about you stores a hypothetical plan as a confirmed fact. You said "I might move to Seattle"; it heard "I live in Seattle." Like a friend who writes in ink what you only said in pencil.
Mixture-of-experts (MoE)
A model design where most of the parameters sit idle at any one moment. Each incoming request is routed to a specialist subset of the network. You get a very large total capacity at a fraction of the running cost of a fully dense model.
Active parameters
The number of weights that actually fire when the model processes a given input, as opposed to the total stored. In MAI-Base-2: 80 billion active out of 2.8 trillion total.
Pre-training vs. post-training
Pre-training is the expensive first phase: running a massive dataset through the model to build up general knowledge and language ability. Post-training then shapes its behavior — making it helpful, safe, and good at specific tasks. Microsoft finished the first phase this week.
Pending queue
In AgentMemGate: a holding area for speculative statements. Nothing here affects the model's memory until the user later confirms or abandons the plan.
GB200
Nvidia's current-generation data-centre chip (part of the Blackwell family). Microsoft used 16,000 of them for MAI-Base-2's training run.
